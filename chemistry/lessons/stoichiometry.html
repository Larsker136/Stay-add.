3.1Stoichiometry

Formulae, equations and relative masses

A formula tells you which atoms are in a substance and how many; a balanced equation tells you how many of each particle react.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

3.1.1

Formulae

  • the molecular formula gives the number and type of each atom in one molecule, e.g. C₂H₆
  • the empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms, e.g. CH₃ for C₂H₆
  • for ionic compounds use the charges of the ions so that the total charge is zero, e.g. Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ → MgCl₂
  • learn the group ions: NH₄⁺ ammonium, OH⁻ hydroxide, NO₃⁻ nitrate, SO₄²⁻ sulfate, CO₃²⁻ carbonate — use brackets when there is more than one, e.g. Ca(OH)₂
3.1.2

Writing equations

  • a word equation names the reactants and products; a symbol equation uses formulae
  • balance an equation by changing only the numbers in front of formulae, never the formulae themselves, so there are the same number of each atom on both sides
  • add state symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous — dissolved in water
  • Supp. ionic equations show only the ions that react, e.g. H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)

3.1.3

Relative masses

  • relative atomic mass Ar: the average mass of the isotopes of an element compared with 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom
  • relative formula mass Mr: the sum of the Ar values of all the atoms in the formula, e.g. Mr of H₂O = 2 × 1 + 16 = 18
  • neither has units because they are relative

3.2Stoichiometry

The mole and the Avogadro constant

Chemists count particles by weighing them. The mole links a mass you can measure to a number of particles you can’t.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

3.2.1

The mole

  • the mole (mol) is the unit of amount of substance
  • one mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules or ions) — the Avogadro constant
  • the mass of one mole of a substance is its Ar or Mr in grams

moles = mass ÷ Mr  ·  n = m ÷ Mr

3.2.2

Reacting masses

  • use the balanced equation: convert the known mass to moles, use the mole ratio, then convert back to mass
  • the substance that is used up first is the limiting reactant; the other is in excess
  • Supp. a full calculation: mass → moles → ratio → moles → mass
3.2.3

Gas volumes and concentrations

  • one mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³) at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.)
  • concentration can be in g/dm³ or mol/dm³; 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • Supp. in a titration calculation, find moles of the solution of known concentration, use the ratio, then the unknown concentration

volume of gas = moles × 24 dm³  ·  concentration = moles ÷ volume in dm³

3.2.4

Empirical formulae, yield and purity Supp.

  • to find an empirical formula from masses: divide each mass by Ar, then divide by the smallest answer to get the whole-number ratio
  • molecular formula = empirical formula × (Mr ÷ Mr of the empirical formula)
  • percentage yield = actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100
  • percentage purity = mass of pure product ÷ mass of impure product × 100
