9.1Metals

Properties, uses and alloys

Metals conduct, bend and shine. Mix in a few atoms of another element and you can make them harder, stronger or rust-proof.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

9.1.1

Physical and chemical properties

  • physical: good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable and ductile, shiny, usually high melting and boiling points
  • chemical: react with oxygen to form oxides (basic), many react with water or steam and with dilute acids to give hydrogen
  • compare with non-metals: poor conductors (except graphite), brittle, dull, often low melting points
9.1.2

Uses of metals

MetalUseBecause it is…
aluminiumaircraft, food containers, overhead cableslow density, resists corrosion, good conductor
copperelectrical wiringvery good conductor, ductile
mild steelcar bodies, machinerystrong, cheap
stainless steelcutlery, chemical planthard, resists rusting
9.1.3

Alloys

  • an alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements, e.g. brass (copper and zinc), stainless steel (iron with chromium, nickel and carbon)
  • alloys can be harder and stronger than pure metals and more resistant to corrosion
  • Supp. in a pure metal the atoms are the same size and the layers slide easily; in an alloy, atoms of a different size disrupt the layers, so they cannot slide as easily

9.2Metals

The reactivity series and corrosion

Some metals explode in water; others sit unchanged for thousands of years. Ranking them explains displacement, rusting and how to stop it.

1

Understand

Two sections and a checklist

9.2.1

The reactivity series

  • potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, (carbon), zinc, iron, (hydrogen), copper, silver, gold
  • the order comes from reactions with cold water (→ hydroxide + hydrogen), steam (→ oxide + hydrogen) and dilute acids (→ salt + hydrogen)
  • a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt, e.g. Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
  • Supp. reactivity depends on how easily a metal loses electrons to form a positive ion
  • Supp. aluminium seems unreactive because of its tough protective oxide layer
9.2.2

Rusting

  • rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide; iron and steel rust only when both water and oxygen are present
  • barrier methods stop water and oxygen reaching the iron: paint, grease or oil, plastic coating
  • Supp. galvanising (a zinc coating) and sacrificial protection (blocks of zinc or magnesium on ships’ hulls) work even when scratched: the more reactive metal loses electrons and corrodes instead of the iron

9.3Metals

Getting metals out of their ores

Most metals are locked in compounds. How you get them out depends on one question: is the metal more reactive than carbon?

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

9.3.1

Extraction and reactivity

  • an ore is a rock containing a metal compound; extraction is a reduction
  • metals below carbon in the reactivity series are extracted by heating the oxide with carbon (e.g. iron, zinc)
  • metals above carbon are extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound (e.g. aluminium)
  • unreactive metals like gold are found as the element
9.3.2

Iron from hematite

  • the blast furnace is fed with hematite (Fe₂O₃), coke (carbon) and limestone; hot air is blown in
  • C + O₂ → CO₂ (heats the furnace); CO₂ + C → 2CO; Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂
  • Supp. limestone decomposes, CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, and CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ removes sandy impurities as slag
9.3.3

Aluminium from bauxite

  • aluminium oxide (from bauxite) is dissolved in molten cryolite and electrolysed
  • aluminium forms at the cathode, oxygen at the carbon anodes
  • Supp. cryolite lowers the melting point, saving energy; the anodes react with oxygen to make CO₂ and must be replaced
