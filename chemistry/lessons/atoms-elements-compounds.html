2.1Atoms, elements & compounds

Inside the atom

An element is one kind of atom. Its proton number decides what it is; its electrons, arranged in shells, decide how it reacts.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

2.1.1

Elements, compounds and mixtures

  • an element is a substance made of only one type of atom (e.g. oxygen, iron)
  • a compound is a substance made of two or more elements chemically combined (e.g. water, H₂O); it has different properties from its elements and can only be separated by a chemical reaction
  • a mixture is two or more substances not chemically combined; it can be separated by physical methods (filtration, distillation, chromatography)
2.1.2

Atomic structure

ParticleWhereRelative massRelative charge
protonin the nucleus1+1
neutronin the nucleus10
electronin shells around the nucleus1/1840−1
  • proton number (atomic number) Z: the number of protons in the nucleus — elements in the Periodic Table are in order of Z
  • mass number (nucleon number) A: the total number of protons and neutrons
  • in an atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the atom is neutral
2.1.3

Electronic configuration

  • electrons fill shells from the inside: the first shell holds up to 2, the second up to 8, the third up to 8 (for the first 20 elements)
  • written like 2,8,1 for sodium and drawn as dots or crosses on circles
  • the number of outer-shell electrons = the group number (for groups I–VII); the number of occupied shells = the period number
  • noble gases (group VIII) have a full outer shell, which makes them unreactive
2.1.4

Isotopes

  • isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons, e.g. chlorine-35 and chlorine-37
  • isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties because they have the same number of outer electrons
  • Supp. the relative atomic mass Ar is the average mass of the isotopes compared with 1/12 of a carbon-12 atom, weighted by their abundance: e.g. chlorine = (75 × 35 + 25 × 37) ÷ 100 = 35.5

2.2Atoms, elements & compounds

Ions and ionic bonds

Metals lose electrons and non-metals gain them. The positive and negative ions that result hold each other tightly in a giant lattice.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

2.2.1

Forming ions

  • an ion is an electrically charged particle formed when an atom loses or gains electrons
  • metal atoms lose their outer electrons → positive ions (cations); e.g. Na 2,8,1 → Na⁺ 2,8
  • non-metal atoms gain electrons to fill their outer shell → negative ions (anions); e.g. Cl 2,8,7 → Cl⁻ 2,8,8
  • the charge of a simple ion follows the group: I → 1+, II → 2+, III → 3+, V → 3−, VI → 2−, VII → 1−
2.2.2

Ionic bonds and lattices

  • an ionic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions
  • ionic bonds form between metals and non-metals; draw them with dot-and-cross diagrams showing only the ions’ outer shells or all shells, with the charge outside square brackets
  • Supp. ionic compounds form a giant lattice: a regular, repeating arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions in three dimensions (e.g. sodium chloride)
2.2.3

Properties of ionic compounds

  • high melting and boiling points — lots of energy is needed to overcome the strong attraction between the ions
  • conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions are free to move; not when solid, because the ions are held in fixed positions
  • often soluble in water

2.3Atoms, elements & compounds

Sharing electrons: covalent bonds

Non-metal atoms can’t pass electrons to each other, so they share them. The result is a molecule — and molecules hold on to each other only weakly.

1

Understand

Two sections and a checklist

2.3.1

Covalent bonds

  • a covalent bond forms when a pair of electrons is shared between two atoms, giving each atom a full outer shell (a noble-gas configuration)
  • covalent bonds form between non-metal atoms
  • one shared pair = a single bond; two = a double bond (O₂, CO₂, C₂H₄); three = a triple bond (N₂)
  • learn the dot-and-cross diagrams of H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, NH₃ and HCl; Supp. also O₂, N₂, CO₂, C₂H₄ and CH₃OH
2.3.2

Properties of simple molecular substances

  • low melting and boiling points: only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules must be overcome — the covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break
  • many are gases or liquids at room temperature
  • poor electrical conductivity: there are no ions and no free electrons to carry charge
  • Supp. melting points rise as molecules get larger, because the intermolecular forces get stronger

2.4Atoms, elements & compounds

Giant structures

Some substances aren’t made of small molecules at all: billions of atoms bonded in one network. Their properties come straight from that structure.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

2.4.1

Diamond and graphite

DiamondGraphite
Bondingeach C bonded to 4 others in a tetrahedral networkeach C bonded to 3 others in flat layers of hexagons
Hardnessvery hardsoft — layers slide over each other (weak forces between layers)
Conductivitydoes not conduct (no free electrons)conducts — one delocalised electron per atom moves along the layers
Usescutting toolselectrodes, lubricant
  • both have very high melting points because many strong covalent bonds must be broken
2.4.2

Silicon(IV) oxide Supp.

  • silicon(IV) oxide (silicon dioxide, SiO₂, found as sand) has a giant covalent structure similar to diamond
  • each silicon atom is bonded to 4 oxygen atoms and each oxygen atom to 2 silicon atoms
  • hard, very high melting point, does not conduct electricity
2.4.3

Metallic bonding

  • a metal is a giant lattice of positive ions in a ‘sea’ of delocalised electrons
  • Supp. metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons
  • good electrical conductors: the delocalised electrons move through the structure
  • malleable and ductile: layers of ions slide over each other without the bonding breaking
  • usually high melting points
