4.1Electrochemistry

Splitting compounds with electricity

Pass a current through a molten or dissolved ionic compound and it breaks apart: metals or hydrogen at one electrode, non-metals at the other.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

4.1.1

What happens in electrolysis

  • electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current
  • the electrolyte is the molten or dissolved ionic compound; the ions must be free to move — a solid ionic compound does not conduct
  • the cathode is the negative electrode: positive ions (cations) move to it and gain electrons (reduction)
  • the anode is the positive electrode: negative ions (anions) move to it and lose electrons (oxidation)
  • in the external circuit, charge is carried by electrons; in the electrolyte, by ions
  • inert electrodes (graphite or platinum) do not react
4.1.2

Molten compounds

  • a molten binary compound gives the metal at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode
  • molten lead(II) bromide → lead at the cathode, bromine (red-brown vapour) at the anode
4.1.3

Aqueous solutions

  • water provides H⁺ and OH⁻ ions as well as the ions of the dissolved compound
  • cathode: the metal is deposited if it is less reactive than hydrogen (e.g. copper); otherwise hydrogen is given off
  • anode: a halogen is given off from a concentrated halide (e.g. chlorine from concentrated sodium chloride); otherwise oxygen is given off
  • concentrated aqueous sodium chloride → hydrogen, chlorine, and sodium hydroxide left in solution; dilute sulfuric acid → hydrogen and oxygen
  • aqueous copper(II) sulfate with carbon electrodes → copper and oxygen; with copper electrodes the anode dissolves and copper is deposited at the cathode (purifying copper)
  • Supp. with dilute aqueous sodium chloride oxygen, not chlorine, forms at the anode
4.1.4

Ionic half-equations Supp.

  • half-equations show what happens at each electrode, with electrons e⁻
  • cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu  ·  2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂
  • anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻  ·  4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻  ·  Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻

4.2Electrochemistry

Electroplating and fuel cells

The same chemistry coats a spoon in silver — and, run in reverse, a fuel cell turns hydrogen and oxygen into electricity.

1

Understand

Two sections and a checklist

4.2.1

Electroplating

  • electroplating coats a metal object with a thin layer of another metal by electrolysis
  • the object is the cathode; the plating metal is the anode; the electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal (e.g. silver nitrate for silver)
  • used to improve appearance and to resist corrosion (e.g. chromium on taps, silver on cutlery, tin on food cans)
4.2.2

Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells

  • a fuel cell uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity, with water as the only product: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
  • advantages compared with petrol engines: no carbon dioxide or other pollutants at the point of use, more efficient, quieter
  • disadvantages: hydrogen is difficult and dangerous to store and transport; it is often made using fossil fuels; fuel cells are expensive
