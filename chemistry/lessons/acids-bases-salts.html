7.1Acids, bases & salts

Acids, bases and pH

Acids make hydrogen ions in water; alkalis make hydroxide ions. When the two meet they cancel out to make water — neutralisation.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

7.1.1

Properties of acids

  • acids turn blue litmus red, methyl orange red and thymolphthalein colourless; pH below 7
  • acid + metal → salt + hydrogen (e.g. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂)
  • acid + base → salt + water (neutralisation)
  • acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide (fizzing)
  • acids release H⁺ ions in water
7.1.2

Bases and alkalis

  • a base neutralises an acid (metal oxides and hydroxides); an alkali is a base that dissolves in water and releases OH⁻ ions
  • alkalis turn red litmus blue, methyl orange yellow and thymolphthalein blue; pH above 7
  • alkali + acid → salt + water; alkali + ammonium salt → salt + water + ammonia
  • neutralisation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)
7.1.3

pH and indicators

  • the pH scale (0–14) measures acidity or alkalinity; pH 7 is neutral
  • universal indicator shows the approximate pH by its colour; a pH meter gives an accurate value
  • the lower the pH, the higher the concentration of H⁺ ions
  • Supp. acids are proton donors and bases are proton acceptors
  • Supp. a strong acid (hydrochloric) is completely ionised in water; a weak acid (ethanoic) is only partially ionised
7.1.4

Oxides

  • metal oxides are usually basic; non-metal oxides are usually acidic
  • Supp. amphoteric oxides such as aluminium oxide and zinc oxide react with both acids and bases to make a salt and water
  • a few oxides (such as carbon monoxide) are neutral

7.2Acids, bases & salts

Making salts

A salt is what you get when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal or ammonium. How you make one depends on whether it dissolves.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

7.2.1

Salts and solubility

  • a salt is formed when the hydrogen ions of an acid are replaced by metal ions or ammonium ions — e.g. hydrochloric acid → chlorides, sulfuric acid → sulfates, nitric acid → nitrates
  • soluble: all sodium, potassium and ammonium salts; all nitrates; chlorides except silver and lead; sulfates except barium, calcium and lead
  • insoluble: carbonates and hydroxides, except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium (calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble)
7.2.2

Preparing soluble salts

  • excess insoluble solid (metal, base or carbonate) + acid → filter off the excess → heat the filtrate to the crystallisation point → cool → filter and dry the crystals
  • titration when both reactants are soluble (an alkali and an acid): find the exact volumes with an indicator, repeat without the indicator, then crystallise
7.2.3

Preparing insoluble salts

  • precipitation: mix two solutions that between them contain the ions of the salt
  • filter off the precipitate, wash it with distilled water, then dry it
  • e.g. barium chloride + sodium sulfate → barium sulfate (white precipitate) + sodium chloride
7.2.4

Hydrated and anhydrous salts

  • a hydrated substance is chemically combined with water; an anhydrous substance contains no water
  • water of crystallisation is the water in the crystal structure, e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue) and CoCl₂·6H₂O (pink)
  • Supp. the formula of a hydrated salt can be found by heating to constant mass and comparing the moles of salt and water
