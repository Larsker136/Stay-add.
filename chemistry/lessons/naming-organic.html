Topic 11Organic chemistry

Naming, part 1: the main chain

Naming is a seven-step routine. The first three steps give you the core of the name: how long the chain is, which family it belongs to, and where the group sits.

1

Understand

Steps 1 to 3

Steps 1 and 2: the chain and the family

Start with the carbon skeleton. The longest unbroken chain of carbon atoms gives the root of the name, and the functional group gives its ending.

  • Step 1. Count the carbons in the longest chain — it may bend on paper. The count picks the root: meth 1 · eth 2 · prop 3 · but 4 · pent 5 · hex 6
  • Step 2. Find the functional group. Its suffix tells you the homologous series: -ane alkane · -ene alkene · -ol alcohol · -oic acid carboxylic acid

A homologous series is a family with the same functional group and the same general formula, so its members react in the same way.

pent (5 carbons) + ol (–OH) → pentanol

Step 3: number the chain

Number the carbons starting from the end that gives the functional group the smallest possible number. That number sits just before the suffix.

  • –OH on the fourth carbon from the left of a five-carbon chain is on the second carbon from the right: pentan-2-ol, not pentan-4-ol
  • in a carboxylic acid the carbon of –COOH is always carbon 1, so the name needs no number
  • with no functional group (an alkane), number so that the side chains get the smallest numbers — part 2
  • when there is only one possible place, the number is left out: ethanol, propene

CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₂CH₃ → pentan-2-ol

Topic 11Organic chemistry

Naming, part 2: side chains

Branches and halogen atoms go at the front of the name. Four more steps decide how they are numbered, grouped, ordered and punctuated.

1

Understand

Steps 4 to 7

Steps 4 and 5: name and count the side chains

Anything attached to the main chain that is not part of it is a side chain. Each goes in front of the main name, with the number of the carbon it is attached to.

  • Step 4. Alkyl side chains end in -yl: –CH₃ methyl, –CH₂CH₃ ethyl. Halogen atoms count as side chains too: F fluoro, Cl chloro, Br bromo, I iodo
  • Step 5. When the same side chain appears more than once, write it once with a multiplier — di (2), tri (3), tetra (4) — and give one number for each

If there is no functional group, number the chain so the side chains get the smallest numbers (step 3).

CH₃CH(CH₃)CH(CH₃)CH₃ → 2,3-dimethylbutane

Steps 6 and 7: order and punctuation

  • Step 6. List different side chains in alphabetical order of their names — ignore di, tri and tetra when sorting: chloro before methyl, ethyl before methyl
  • if both ends give the same numbers, the side chain that comes first in the alphabet gets the smaller number
  • Step 7. Use a comma between two numbers (2,3) and a hyphen between a number and a letter (2-chloro). The last side chain joins the main name with no gap: 2-methylbutane

2-chloro-4-methylpentane · 2,2-dimethylpropane · 3-ethylpentane

Topic 11Organic chemistry

Pick the right name

Every wrong name breaks one of the seven steps. Spot which step, and the right name follows.

1

Understand

The seven steps as a checklist

Check a name in seven steps

Read any name back against the drawing, one step at a time, and you will catch almost every mistake.

  • 1 Is the root the longest chain?
  • 2 Does the suffix match the functional group?
  • 3 Was the chain numbered from the end that gives the smallest numbers?
  • 4 Does every side chain have a name and a number?
  • 5 Are repeated side chains joined with di/tri?
  • 6 Are the side chains in alphabetical order?
  • 7 Commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and letters?

The usual traps

  • Step 1: a –CH₂CH₃ drawn at the end of a chain is part of the chain — “2-ethylpropane” is really 2-methylbutane
  • Step 3: numbering from the wrong end — “4-methylpentane” should be 2-methylpentane
  • Step 5: writing the same side chain twice — “2-methyl-3-methylbutane” is 2,3-dimethylbutane
  • Step 6: putting methyl before chloro because its number is smaller
  • Step 7: “2-3-dimethyl” or “2,3,dimethyl” instead of 2,3-dimethyl

Topic 11Organic chemistry

Isomers and esters

Same atoms, different arrangement — and how to name the sweet-smelling compounds made from an alcohol and an acid.

1

Understand

Structural isomers, and the two-word ester name

Structural isomers Supp.

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but a different structural formula.

  • but-1-ene and but-2-ene are both C₄H₈ — the C=C is in a different place
  • propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol are both C₃H₈O — the –OH is on a different carbon
  • butane and 2-methylpropane are both C₄H₁₀ — one chain is branched

Try them in the builders: change only the position, or move a carbon into a side chain, and watch the molecular formula stay the same.

CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ and CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₃ → both C₄H₁₀

Naming esters Supp.

An ester forms when a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol, with an acid catalyst. Water is given off.

Name it in two words: the alcohol part first, ending in -yl, then the acid part, ending in -oate.

ethanol + ethanoic acid → ethyl ethanoate + water

Careful: the name starts with the alcohol, but the formula is usually written acid part first: CH₃COOCH₂CH₃.
