6.1Chemical reactions

How fast? Rates of reaction

Reactions happen when particles collide hard enough. Anything that makes those collisions more frequent or more energetic speeds the reaction up.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

6.1.1

Physical and chemical changes

  • a physical change makes no new substance and is usually easy to reverse (melting, dissolving)
  • a chemical change makes new substances and is usually hard to reverse; often there is a temperature change, a colour change, fizzing or a precipitate
6.1.2

Factors affecting the rate

Increase…RateWhy (collision theory)
concentration of a solutionincreasesmore particles in the same volume → more frequent collisions
pressure of a gasincreasesparticles closer together → more frequent collisions
surface area of a solid (smaller pieces)increasesmore particles exposed → more frequent collisions
temperatureincreasesfaster particles: more frequent collisions and more with energy ≥ Eₐ
a catalystincreasesan alternative pathway with lower Eₐ; the catalyst is not used up
6.1.3

Collision theory

  • particles must collide with at least the activation energy to react — a successful collision
  • the rate depends on the number of successful collisions per unit time (the collision frequency)
  • Supp. temperature has two effects: particles have more kinetic energy, so they collide more often and a greater proportion of collisions have energy above Eₐ
  • enzymes are biological catalysts

To watch individual collisions, open the Collision theory lab.

6.1.4

Measuring and interpreting rate

  • measure the volume of gas given off (gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder) or the loss of mass (gas escaping from a flask on a balance), at regular time intervals
  • for a precipitate, time how long until a cross under the flask disappears
  • on a graph of product against time, the gradient is the rate: steepest at the start, zero when a reactant is used up
  • the final amount of product depends on the amount of the limiting reactant, not on the rate

6.2Chemical reactions

Reversible reactions and equilibrium

Some reactions run both ways at once. In a closed system they settle into a balance — and industry tunes the conditions to tip that balance.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

6.2.1

Reversible reactions

  • a reversible reaction can go in both directions, shown by ⇌
  • heating hydrated copper(II) sulfate (blue) gives anhydrous copper(II) sulfate (white) and water; adding water turns it blue again — a test for water
  • the same works with hydrated cobalt(II) chloride (pink ⇌ blue)
6.2.2

Equilibrium

  • in a closed system a reversible reaction reaches dynamic equilibrium: the rates of the forward and reverse reactions are equal and the concentrations no longer change
  • Supp. changing the conditions shifts the position of equilibrium to oppose the change: higher temperature favours the endothermic direction; higher pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules; adding a reactant favours the products
  • Supp. a catalyst speeds up both directions equally — equilibrium is reached sooner, but its position does not change
6.2.3

The Haber process

  • makes ammonia: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) — nitrogen from the air, hydrogen from methane
  • conditions: about 450 °C, 200 atm, an iron catalyst
  • Supp. a lower temperature would give a higher yield (forward reaction exothermic) but too slow a rate; a higher pressure gives a higher yield but costs more and is more dangerous — the conditions are a compromise
6.2.4

The Contact process

  • makes sulfuric acid; the key step is 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g)
  • sulfur dioxide from burning sulfur or roasting sulfide ores; oxygen from the air
  • conditions: about 450 °C, 1–2 atm, a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst
  • Supp. the yield is already high at low pressure, so higher pressure is not worth the cost

6.3Chemical reactions

Oxidation and reduction

Electrons lost by one substance are gained by another. Oxidation and reduction always happen together — redox.

1

Understand

Two sections and a checklist

6.3.1

Oxidation and reduction

  • oxidation is the gain of oxygen; reduction is the loss of oxygen — e.g. in CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, copper(II) oxide is reduced and hydrogen is oxidised
  • a reaction in which both happen is a redox reaction
  • Roman numerals show the charge of a metal ion: iron(II) is Fe²⁺, iron(III) is Fe³⁺
6.3.2

Electron transfer and oxidation numbers Supp.

  • oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain of electrons — OIL RIG
  • oxidation is an increase in oxidation number; reduction is a decrease
  • an element has oxidation number 0; a simple ion has the same number as its charge; in a compound the numbers add up to zero
  • an oxidising agent oxidises another substance and is itself reduced; a reducing agent reduces another substance and is itself oxidised
  • acidified potassium manganate(VII) turns from purple to colourless with a reducing agent; potassium iodide turns from colourless to brown with an oxidising agent
