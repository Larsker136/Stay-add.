5.1Nuclear physics

A tiny, dense nucleus

Almost all of an atom’s mass is packed into a nucleus a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom itself. The protons inside decide what element it is.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

5.1.1

The atom

  • an atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus (protons and neutrons) surrounded by negative electrons
  • atoms are neutral: the number of electrons equals the number of protons; losing or gaining electrons makes ions
  • Supp. the alpha-particle scattering experiment: most alpha particles went straight through gold foil (the atom is mostly empty space); a few were deflected through large angles (a tiny, dense, positive nucleus)
5.1.2

  • proton number Z: the number of protons; nucleon number A: protons + neutrons; neutrons = A − Z
  • nuclide notation: AZX, e.g. 146C
  • isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A); some are unstable
5.1.3

Fission and fusion Supp.

  • nuclear fission: a large nucleus (e.g. uranium-235) absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy and more neutrons — a chain reaction in nuclear reactors
  • nuclear fusion: two light nuclei join to make a heavier nucleus, releasing energy — the energy source of the Sun and stars

5.2Nuclear physics

Unstable nuclei, random decay

An unstable nucleus gives out radiation to become more stable. When any one nucleus decays is pure chance — but a large sample halves in a predictable time.

1

Understand

Five sections and a checklist

5.2.1

Detecting radioactivity

  • ionising radiation is detected with a Geiger–Müller tube and counter, or photographic film; the count rate is counts per second or per minute
  • background radiation is always present: radon gas from rocks, buildings and the ground, food and drink, cosmic rays, medical and nuclear sources
  • always subtract the background count from a measured count rate

5.2.3

Radioactive decay

  • decay is a change in an unstable nucleus that is spontaneous and random
  • alpha: A falls by 4, Z falls by 2 · beta: a neutron becomes a proton and an electron; A unchanged, Z rises by 1 · gamma: no change in A or Z
  • isotopes with too many neutrons tend to beta-decay; very heavy nuclei tend to alpha-decay
5.2.4

Half-life

  • the half-life of an isotope is the time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in a sample to decay
  • find it from a decay curve: read the time for the count rate to fall to half (after subtracting background)
  • uses depend on half-life: tracers in medicine need a short half-life; carbon-14 (5700 years) dates ancient remains; smoke alarms use a long-lived alpha source
5.2.5

Safety

  • ionising radiation can damage cells and DNA, causing mutations and cancer
  • reduce exposure: keep the time short, the distance large (use tongs), use shielding (lead), and store sources in lead-lined boxes
