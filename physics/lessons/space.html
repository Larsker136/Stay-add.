6.1Space physics

Our home in the Solar System

Days, months, years and seasons all come from three motions: the Earth spinning, the Moon orbiting the Earth, and the Earth orbiting the Sun.

1

Understand

Two sections and a checklist

6.1.1

The Earth and the Moon

  • the Earth spins on its axis once every 24 hours → day and night, and the apparent daily motion of the Sun
  • the Earth orbits the Sun once in about 365 days → a year
  • the Earth’s axis is tilted, so as it orbits, each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for part of the year → seasons
  • the Moon orbits the Earth about once a month (about 27 days), and its phases come from how much of its sunlit side we can see
6.1.2

The Solar System

  • the Sun, eight planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune), dwarf planets such as Pluto, moons, asteroids and comets
  • the four inner planets are small and rocky; the four outer planets are large gas giants — because the Solar System formed from a rotating cloud of gas and dust (an accretion disc), where only rock could condense near the hot young Sun
  • the Sun’s gravitational attraction keeps the planets in orbit; the orbits are almost circular (comets have very elliptical orbits)
  • Supp. the further a planet is from the Sun, the weaker the Sun’s gravitational field, the lower its orbital speed and the longer its orbital period

average orbital speed v = 2πr / T

Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach the Earth (speed of light 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s).

6.2Space physics

Stars are born, shine and die

The Sun is an ordinary star, powered by fusion. Its future depends on its mass — and the light from distant galaxies tells us the whole Universe is expanding.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

6.2.1

The Sun as a star

  • the Sun is a medium-sized star, made mostly of hydrogen and helium
  • it radiates most of its energy as infrared, visible light and ultraviolet
  • stars are powered by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium in their cores
6.2.2

The life cycle of stars Supp.

  1. a nebula (cloud of dust and gas) collapses under gravity to form a protostar, which heats up
  2. when fusion starts it becomes a stable main sequence star: inward gravity balances outward pressure from the hot core
  3. when core hydrogen runs out it swells into a red giant (or a red supergiant if very massive)
  4. a Sun-like star sheds a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf
  5. a massive star explodes as a supernova, leaving a neutron star or, if very massive, a black hole; the debris forms new nebulae with heavier elements
6.2.3

Galaxies and the Universe

  • the Sun is one of billions of stars in the Milky Way galaxy; the Universe contains billions of galaxies
  • the light-year is the distance light travels in one year (about 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m); the Milky Way is about 100 000 light-years across
  • redshift: light from distant galaxies has longer wavelengths than expected — they are moving away; more distant galaxies have bigger redshifts
  • this, and the cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR), supports the Big Bang theory: the Universe began expanding from a tiny point about 13.8 billion years ago
  • Supp. Hubble constant H₀ = v / d ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ per second; the age of the Universe ≈ 1 / H₀
