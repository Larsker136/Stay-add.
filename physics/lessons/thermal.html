2.1Thermal physics

Everything is made of moving particles

Solids, liquids and gases are the same particles arranged differently. Heat them and they move faster — which explains melting, gas pressure and absolute zero.

2.1.2

The particle model and Brownian motion

  • increasing the temperature increases the average kinetic energy of the particles
  • Brownian motion: tiny visible particles (smoke in air, pollen in water) jitter randomly because they are hit by fast-moving, invisible molecules — evidence for the particle model
  • Supp. the visible particles move randomly because the much smaller molecules hit them unevenly from all sides
2.1.3

Gases, pressure and absolute zero

  • gas pressure is caused by particles colliding with the walls; each collision exerts a force, and pressure = force per unit area
  • at constant volume, higher temperature → faster particles → more frequent and harder collisions → higher pressure
  • at constant temperature, a smaller volume → more frequent collisions → higher pressure
  • absolute zero (−273 °C, 0 K) is the temperature at which particles have the least possible kinetic energy

T (K) = θ (°C) + 273 · Supp. pV = constant (fixed mass of gas at constant temperature)

2.2Thermal physics

Expanding, warming, melting, boiling

Heating makes things expand and get hotter — until they change state, when the temperature stops rising while the energy breaks bonds instead.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

2.2.1

Thermal expansion

  • solids, liquids and gases expand when heated; gases expand most, solids least
  • uses and problems: liquid-in-glass thermometers, gaps in railway tracks and bridges, bimetallic strips in thermostats, overhead cables sagging in summer
  • Supp. particles move more and further apart; in a gas the particles are far apart and the forces between them very weak, so it expands most
2.2.2

Specific heat capacity

  • a rise in temperature means an increase in the particles’ average kinetic energy
  • Supp. specific heat capacity c is the energy needed per unit mass per unit temperature increase

ΔE = m c Δθ · c(water) = 4200 J/(kg °C)

Measuring c

Heat a known mass of a material with an electric heater (energy = power × time), measure the temperature rise, then c = ΔE / (mΔθ). Insulate to reduce energy losses.

2.2.3

Melting, boiling and evaporation

  • during melting and boiling energy is transferred but the temperature stays constant; pure water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at standard pressure
  • evaporation is when the most energetic particles escape from the surface of a liquid — it happens at any temperature, and cools the liquid left behind (that is how sweating cools you)
  • evaporation is faster with a higher temperature, a larger surface area and air moving over the surface
  • Supp. boiling happens throughout the liquid at a definite temperature; evaporation only at the surface

2.3Thermal physics

Conduction, convection, radiation

Thermal energy moves from hot to cold three ways: passed particle to particle, carried by a moving fluid, or sent out as infrared waves.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

2.3.1

Conduction

  • metals are good thermal conductors; plastics, wood, glass and air are poor conductors (insulators)
  • in all solids, particles vibrate more when heated and pass the vibrations on to neighbours
  • Supp. metals also have free (delocalised) electrons that move through the metal and carry energy quickly
2.3.2

Convection

  • convection happens in fluids (liquids and gases)
  • heated fluid expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place — a convection current
  • examples: heating water in a kettle, sea breezes, a radiator warming a room
2.3.3

Radiation

  • thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation; it needs no medium — it reaches us from the Sun through space
  • dull black surfaces are the best emitters and absorbers; shiny white/silver surfaces are the worst emitters and the best reflectors
  • hotter objects, and objects with a larger surface area, emit more radiation
  • Supp. an object at constant temperature absorbs and emits radiation at the same rate; the Earth’s temperature depends on the balance between incoming and emitted radiation
2.3.4

Consequences of energy transfer

  • cooking pans: metal base to conduct, plastic handle to insulate
  • radiators and heaters: convection currents warm the room; heaters at the bottom, freezer compartments at the top
  • a vacuum flask reduces all three: vacuum (conduction, convection), silvered walls (radiation), stopper (convection)
  • houses: loft insulation and cavity walls trap air; double glazing
