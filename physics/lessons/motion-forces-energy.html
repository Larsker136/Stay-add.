1.1Motion, forces & energy

Measure it, then say which way

Physics starts with careful measurement. Some quantities only have a size; others — like force and velocity — also have a direction, and that changes how you add them.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

1.1.1

Measuring length, volume and time

  • rulers and measuring tapes measure length; read at eye level to avoid parallax error
  • measuring cylinders measure liquid volume — read the bottom of the meniscus
  • stopwatches and clocks measure time intervals
  • to measure something very small or short, measure many and divide: the thickness of one sheet = thickness of 100 sheets ÷ 100; the period of a pendulum = time for 20 swings ÷ 20
1.1.2

Scalars and vectors

ScalarVector
Hassize (magnitude) onlysize and direction
Examplesdistance, speed, time, mass, energy, temperatureforce, weight, velocity, acceleration, momentum, electric field strength, gravitational field strength
1.1.3

Adding vectors at right angles Supp.

  • draw the vectors tip to tail; the resultant goes from the start of the first to the end of the last
  • for two vectors at right angles, use Pythagoras for the size and trigonometry for the angle — or a scale drawing

R = √(F₁² + F₂²) · angle θ = tan⁻¹(F₂ / F₁)

1.2Motion, forces & energy

Speed, velocity and acceleration

How far, how fast, and how quickly the speed changes — and the two graphs that show it all at a glance.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

1.2.1

Speed and velocity

speed = distance ÷ time · v = s / t

  • average speed = total distance ÷ total time
  • velocity is speed in a given direction — a vector
1.2.2

Acceleration

acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken · a = Δv / Δt

  • measured in m/s²; a negative acceleration is a deceleration
  • near the Earth’s surface, an object falling with no air resistance accelerates at g ≈ 9.8 m/s²
1.2.3

Distance–time and speed–time graphs

GraphGradient meansShapes
distance–timespeedstraight sloping line: constant speed · flat: at rest · curving upwards: accelerating
speed–timeaccelerationstraight sloping line: constant acceleration · flat: constant speed · area under the line = distance travelled
1.2.4

Falling and terminal velocity Supp.

  1. at first only weight acts, so the object accelerates at g
  2. as it speeds up, air resistance increases, so the resultant force — and the acceleration — get smaller
  3. when air resistance equals weight the resultant force is zero: the object falls at a constant terminal velocity

1.3Motion, forces & energy

Mass is matter; weight is a force

Your mass is the same on the Moon as on Earth. Your weight — the pull of gravity on that mass — is not.

1

Understand

Two sections and a checklist

1.3.1

Mass and weight

  • mass is a measure of the quantity of matter in an object at rest relative to the observer (kg)
  • weight is a gravitational force on an object that has mass (N)
  • weights (and so masses) can be compared using a balance
1.3.2

Gravitational field strength

W = m g · g = W / m

  • g is the gravitational field strength — the force per unit mass (N/kg); on Earth g ≈ 9.8 N/kg
  • g is also the acceleration of free fall (m/s²) — the same number Supp.
  • weight is the effect of a gravitational field on a mass

1.4Motion, forces & energy

Why some things float

A ship of steel floats; a pebble sinks. Density — how much mass is packed into each cubic centimetre — decides.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

1.4.1

Density

density = mass ÷ volume · ρ = m / V

  • units: kg/m³ or g/cm³ (1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³); water is about 1 g/cm³
1.4.2

Measuring density

ObjectMassVolume
liquidbalance: mass of cylinder with liquid − empty cylinderread from the measuring cylinder
regular solidbalancemeasure the sides with a ruler and calculate
irregular solidbalancedisplacement: lower it into water in a measuring cylinder; the rise in level is its volume
1.4.3

Floating and sinking Supp.

  • an object floats in a liquid if it is less dense than the liquid, and sinks if it is denser
  • one liquid floats on another if it is less dense — oil on water

1.5Motion, forces & energy

Forces stretch, turn and accelerate

A resultant force changes how an object moves. Forces can also change its shape — and, if they act off-centre, make it turn.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

1.5.1

Effects of forces

  • forces can change an object’s size and shape, its speed and its direction of motion
  • several forces on an object combine into one resultant; along a straight line, add those in one direction and subtract those in the other
  • if the resultant force is zero, an object stays at rest or keeps moving at a constant velocity
  • friction (and air resistance, drag) acts against motion and heats the surfaces

F = m a Supp. · resultant force = mass × acceleration

Supp. An object moving in a circle at constant speed needs a force towards the centre; a bigger speed, mass or smaller radius needs a bigger force.

1.5.2

Springs and Hooke’s law

  • a load–extension graph is a straight line through the origin while the spring obeys Hooke’s law
  • the limit of proportionality is the point beyond which extension is no longer proportional to load

F = k x Supp. · k = spring constant (N/m), x = extension

1.5.3

Turning effect: moments

moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot

  • a moment is measured in N m; levers, spanners and see-saws use it
  • principle of moments: for an object in equilibrium, sum of clockwise moments = sum of anticlockwise moments
  • for equilibrium there must also be no resultant force
1.5.4

Centre of gravity

  • the centre of gravity is the point where the whole weight of an object seems to act
  • find it for a flat shape by hanging it from different points and drawing plumb lines; they cross at the centre of gravity
  • an object is more stable with a low centre of gravity and a wide base; it topples if its weight acts outside its base

1.6Motion, forces & energy

Momentum is always conserved

In every collision and explosion, the total momentum before equals the total after. That one rule predicts how fast things move once they’ve hit.

1

Understand

Three sections and a checklist

1.6.1

Momentum Supp.

momentum = mass × velocity · p = m v (kg m/s)

  • momentum is a vector — choose one direction as positive
1.6.2

Conservation of momentum Supp.

  • when objects collide or push apart, and no external force acts, the total momentum is conserved
  • if two objects stick together: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = (m₁ + m₂) v
  • in an explosion from rest the total momentum stays zero, so the parts move apart with equal and opposite momentum
1.6.3

Impulse and force Supp.

impulse = F Δt = Δ(m v) · resultant force = change in momentum ÷ time

Crumple zones, airbags and seatbelts make the collision last longer, so for the same change in momentum the force on the person is smaller.

1.7Motion, forces & energy

Energy is stored, transferred — never lost

Energy moves between stores: kinetic, gravitational, chemical, thermal… The total stays the same, but some always ends up spread out as useless heat.

1

Understand

Four sections and a checklist

1.7.1

Energy stores and transfers

  • stores: kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic (strain), nuclear, electrostatic, internal (thermal)
  • transfers: by forces (mechanical work), electrical currents (electrical work), heating, and waves (light, sound)
  • energy is conserved: it can’t be created or destroyed, only transferred

KE = ½ m v² · ΔGPE = m g Δh Supp.

1.7.2

Work done

work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force · W = F d = ΔE

Work is a transfer of energy: when a force moves an object, the work done equals the energy transferred (J).

1.7.3

Most of our energy comes originally from the Sun (fossil fuels, wind, waves, biofuels); not from the Sun: nuclear, geothermal, tides. Supp. The Sun’s energy comes from nuclear fusion.

1.7.4

Efficiency and power

efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input (× 100 %) · power P = W / t = ΔE / t

  • power is the rate of doing work / transferring energy, in watts (1 W = 1 J/s)

1.8Motion, forces & energy

Force spread over an area

A sharp knife cuts, a wide tyre doesn’t sink. And the deeper you dive, the harder the water presses on you.

1

Understand

Two sections and a checklist

1.8.1

Pressure

pressure = force ÷ area · p = F / A (Pa = N/m²)

A larger area spreads the force and lowers the pressure (snowshoes, wide tyres); a smaller area increases it (knives, nails, drawing pins).

1.8.2

Pressure in liquids

  • pressure in a liquid increases with depth and with the density of the liquid
  • at a given depth it acts equally in all directions

Δp = ρ g Δh Supp.
