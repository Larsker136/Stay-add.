13.1Excretion

Getting rid of what metabolism leaves behind

Every cell makes waste: carbon dioxide from respiration, and — from spare amino acids — urea. The lungs and kidneys remove them before they build up to toxic levels.

1

Understand

Three sections

13.1.1

Excretory products

Definition Excretion is the removal of waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements.

SubstanceMade byExcreted by
Carbon dioxiderespiration in all cellslungs
Ureathe liver, from excess amino acidskidneys (in urine)
Excess water and ionsdietkidneys (and some in sweat)

If not removed, these are toxic: CO₂ lowers the pH of the blood, and urea damages cells.

13.1.2

The liver and urea Supp.

  • the body cannot store excess amino acids
  • in the liver they are deaminated: the nitrogen-containing part is removed and turned into urea
  • the rest of the molecule is used for energy or stored as glycogen or fat
  • urea travels in the blood plasma to the kidneys
  • the liver also assimilates amino acids into proteins, such as plasma proteins (including fibrinogen), and breaks down alcohol and other toxins

excess amino acids → (deamination in liver) → urea → blood → kidneys → urine

13.1.3

Summary and key terms

  • CO₂ out via lungs; urea, water and ions out via kidneys
  • liver deaminates excess amino acids to make urea (Supp.)
Excretion
removal of the waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements
Urea

Deamination

13.1Excretion

The urinary system

Two kidneys filter all your blood many times a day. Waste and spare water drain down the ureters to the bladder, and leave through the urethra.

13.1.5

Inside a kidney Supp.

  • the outer layer is the cortex, the inner layer the medulla
  • urine collects in the central space and drains into the ureter
  • each kidney contains about a million tiny tubules called nephrons — the filtering units
13.1.6

What urine contains

  • water, urea and excess ions (salts)
  • healthy urine contains no glucose and no protein
  • its volume and concentration change: after drinking a lot, more dilute urine is made; when dehydrated (hot day, exercise), less, more concentrated urine
13.1.7

Summary and key terms

  • kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra
  • urine: water, urea, ions; no glucose or protein

13.1Excretion

The nephron: filter everything, take back what you need

Each nephron squeezes small molecules out of the blood under pressure, then reabsorbs all the glucose and most of the water. What is left becomes urine.

1

Understand

Three sections

13.1.8

Filtration in the glomerulus Supp.

  • blood arrives at the glomerulus — a knot of capillaries — at high pressure
  • the glomerulus sits inside a cup called the Bowman’s capsule
  • small molecules are forced out of the blood into the capsule: water, glucose, urea, ions — this is the filtrate
  • blood cells and proteins are too large and stay in the blood
13.1.9

Reabsorption in the tubule Supp.

  • as the filtrate flows along the tubule, useful substances are reabsorbed into the surrounding capillaries
  • all the glucose is reabsorbed (in the first part of the tubule), partly by active transport
  • some ions and much of the water are reabsorbed — the amount of water depends on how much the body needs
  • the remaining fluid — water, urea and excess ions — flows down the collecting duct to the ureter as urine

glomerulus (filter) → Bowman’s capsule → tubule (reabsorb glucose, ions, water) → collecting duct → ureter

13.1.10

Summary and key terms

  • filtration: high pressure forces small molecules into the capsule; cells and proteins stay (Supp.)
  • reabsorption: all glucose, some ions, much water return to the blood (Supp.)
Glomerulus
a knot of capillaries in the Bowman’s capsule where filtration happens (Supp.)
Bowman’s capsule

Reabsorption
