20.1Human influences

Feeding eight billion people

Machinery, fertilisers, pesticides and selective breeding have multiplied how much food each field produces. But growing one crop or packing animals tightly comes at a cost.

1

Understand

Four sections

20.1.1

How agriculture produces more food

  • agricultural machinery — larger areas farmed more efficiently
  • chemical fertilisers — supply nitrate and other ions to increase yield
  • insecticides and herbicides — kill pests and weeds that reduce yield
  • selective breeding — higher-yielding, disease-resistant crops and livestock
20.1.2

Monocultures and intensive livestock Supp.

PracticeNegative impacts
Large-scale monocultures (one crop over a huge area)reduced biodiversity; pests and diseases spread easily through identical plants, so more pesticide is used; soil nutrients depleted; habitats removed
Intensive livestock production (animals kept close together indoors)disease spreads quickly; overuse of antibiotics (risking resistance); waste can pollute water; animal welfare concerns; large amounts of crops grown for feed
20.1.3

Why famine still happens Supp.

  • increasing human population and unequal distribution of food
  • drought and flooding destroying crops
  • poverty — people cannot afford food even when it exists
  • war and political instability; transport problems
20.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • machinery, fertilisers, pesticides and selective breeding increase yields
  • monocultures and intensive livestock have costs to biodiversity, health and the environment (Supp.)
Monoculture
growing a single crop over a large area (Supp.)
Fertiliser
a substance added to soil to supply mineral ions for crops
Famine
a severe, widespread shortage of food (Supp.)

20.2Human influences

When the forest goes

Clearing land for farms, houses and mines destroys habitats. Cutting down forests also changes the soil, the water cycle and the air.

1

Understand

Three sections

20.2.1

Why habitats are destroyed

  • clearing land for agriculture and housing
  • extraction of natural resources — mining, quarrying, logging
  • freshwater and marine pollution damaging aquatic habitats
  • food chains and webs are disrupted when a habitat is lost, and biodiversity falls
20.2.2

Effects of deforestation Supp.

  • loss of habitats and reduced biodiversity; some species become extinct
  • soil erosion — no roots to hold the soil, so rain washes it away
  • flooding — less water absorbed by trees and soil; rivers silt up
  • disruption of the water cycle — less transpiration, so less rain locally
  • increased carbon dioxide in the atmosphere — less photosynthesis, and wood is often burnt — contributing to climate change
20.2.3

Summary and key terms

  • habitats lost to farming, housing, extraction and pollution
  • deforestation: habitat loss, extinction, erosion, flooding, less rain, more CO₂ (Supp.)
Biodiversity
the number of different species living in an area
Deforestation
the large-scale removal of forest
Extinction
the permanent loss of all members of a species

20.3Human influences

Pollution: the wrong substance in the wrong place

Fertiliser that grows crops can choke a river; carbon dioxide that plants need can warm the planet. Pollution is about amounts and places.

1

Understand

Five sections

20.3.1

PollutantSourceEffect

Pesticides (insecticides, herbicides)farmingkill non-target species; may build up along food chains

20.3.2

Eutrophication Supp.

  1. fertiliser or sewage gets into water: more nitrate and phosphate ions
  2. increased growth of algae and producers — an algal bloom covers the surface
  3. light is blocked, so plants below cannot photosynthesise and die
  4. more dead organic matter; decomposers (bacteria) multiply
  5. increased aerobic respiration by decomposers uses up the dissolved oxygen
  6. fish and other organisms that need oxygen die
20.3.3

Non-biodegradable plastics

  • plastics are not broken down by decomposers, so they persist for hundreds of years
  • marine: animals get tangled, or swallow plastic mistaken for food and starve; microplastics enter food chains
  • land: litter and landfill; toxic chemicals can leach into soil and water
20.3.4

The greenhouse effect and climate change

  • carbon dioxide and methane are greenhouse gases
  • CO₂ comes from burning fossil fuels and deforestation; methane from cattle, rice fields and landfill
  • Supp. the Sun’s radiation passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth; the Earth gives off longer-wavelength (infrared) radiation, and greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit some of it back, trapping heat
  • more greenhouse gases → an enhanced greenhouse effect → global warming and climate change: melting ice, rising sea levels, extreme weather, changed habitats
20.3.5

Summary and key terms

  • sewage, fertiliser, pesticides, chemicals and plastics pollute water and land
  • eutrophication: nutrients → algae → plants die → decomposers use O₂ → fish die (Supp.)
  • CO₂ and methane → enhanced greenhouse effect → climate change
Eutrophication
excess nutrients in water leading to algal growth and loss of oxygen (Supp.)
Non-biodegradable
not broken down by decomposers
Greenhouse gas
a gas that absorbs heat radiated from the Earth, such as CO₂ or methane

20.4Human influences

Conservation: use it without using it up

A sustainable resource is one we can keep using for ever — if we take no more than can be replaced. Conservation also means protecting the species we could otherwise lose.

1

Understand

Four sections

20.4.1

Sustainable resources

  • a sustainable resource is one which is produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment, so it does not run out
  • fossil fuels are non-renewable: they form over millions of years and will run out
  • Supp. sustainable development balances the needs of people now with protecting the environment for the future — it needs cooperation between local, national and international groups
20.4.2

Conserving forests and fish stocks

ResourceHow it is conserved
Forestseducation; protected areas; replanting — a new tree for each one felled; legal quotas
Fish stockseducation; quotas on how much can be caught; net size controls so young fish escape and can breed; closed seasons; protected areas; monitoring; restocking

Paper, glass, plastic and metal can be recycled to save resources and energy. Sewage is treated so it can be safely returned to rivers.

20.4.3

Endangered species

  • species become endangered or extinct through climate change, habitat destruction, hunting, overharvesting, pollution and introduced species
  • they can be conserved by: monitoring and protecting species and habitats, education, captive breeding programmes, and seed banks
  • Supp. reasons for conservation: to keep biodiversity, protect food chains, conserve species that may give medicines or useful genes, and for ecotourism and ethics
  • Supp. a small population loses genetic variation and is less able to adapt; captive breeding may use artificial insemination and IVF to increase variation between zoos
20.4.4

Summary and key terms

  • sustainable: produced as fast as removed
  • forests: replanting; fish: quotas, net sizes, closed seasons
  • endangered species: protect habitats, captive breeding, seed banks, education
Sustainable resource
a resource produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment so that it does not run out
Captive breeding
breeding endangered animals in zoos to increase numbers and release them
Seed bank
a store of seeds kept to preserve plant species and their genes
