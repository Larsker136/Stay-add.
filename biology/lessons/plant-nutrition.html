6.1Plant nutrition

Photosynthesis: making food from light

Plants build their own glucose from carbon dioxide and water, using light energy trapped by chlorophyll. Almost every food chain on Earth starts here.

1

Understand

Five sections

6.1.1

What photosynthesis is

Definition Photosynthesis is the process by which plants synthesise carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light.

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light, chlorophyll)

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (Supp.)

  • carbon dioxide diffuses in through the stomata; water comes up from the roots in the xylem
  • oxygen is a waste product — it diffuses out through the stomata or is used in respiration
6.1.2

Chlorophyll

  • chlorophyll is the green pigment in chloroplasts
  • it absorbs light energy, mainly red and blue light; green light is reflected, which is why leaves look green
  • it transfers light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose and other molecules Supp.
6.1.3

What a plant does with its glucose

6.1.5

Summary and key terms

  • carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using light absorbed by chlorophyll
  • glucose → respiration, starch, cellulose, sucrose, nectar, proteins, fats
  • nitrate for proteins; magnesium for chlorophyll
Chlorophyll
the green pigment in chloroplasts that absorbs light energy
Limiting factor
something present in short supply that stops a process going faster (see 6.1 Rate)

6.1Plant nutrition

What holds photosynthesis back

Turn up the light and the rate rises — until something else runs short. Experiments with starch tests, pondweed bubbles and indicator colours show what a leaf needs.

1

Understand

Five sections

6.1.6

Testing a leaf for starch

Method

  1. dip the leaf in boiling water for about a minute — kills the cells and breaks the membranes so iodine can get in
  2. put it in hot ethanol (in a water bath — no flames) to remove the chlorophyll, so the colour change is easy to see
  3. dip it in water to soften it, spread it on a white tile and add iodine solution
  4. blue-black = starch present (photosynthesis happened); orange-brown = no starch

First destarch the plant by keeping it in the dark for 48 hours, so any starch found was made during the experiment.

6.1.7

Showing what a leaf needs

NeedExperimentResult
Lightcover part of a destarched leaf with foil, then leave in lightonly the uncovered part turns blue-black
Chlorophylluse a variegated leaf (green and white)only the green parts contain starch
Carbon dioxideenclose a leaf in a bag with soda lime or sodium hydroxide (absorbs CO₂); control bag without itleaf without CO₂ has no starch
6.1.8

Measuring the rate

  • pondweed (Elodea) in water gives off bubbles of oxygen; count the bubbles per minute or collect the gas in a syringe
  • change one variable — distance of a lamp, CO₂ (add sodium hydrogencarbonate), temperature — and keep the others the same
  • moving the lamp closer increases light intensity; a beaker of water between lamp and plant stops the lamp heating it
  • hydrogencarbonate indicator shows gas exchange: purple = low CO₂ (photosynthesis faster than respiration), red = equal, yellow = high CO₂ (in the dark, only respiration)
6.1.9

Limiting factors Supp.

  • the rate depends on light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature
  • a limiting factor is the one in shortest supply — increasing it increases the rate
  • on a graph, where the line rises, the factor on the x-axis is limiting; where it levels off, something else is now limiting
  • temperature affects the enzymes: too high and they denature, so the rate falls
  • growers use this in glasshouses: extra lighting, heaters and burning paraffin to add CO₂ — but only as much as raises yield enough to pay for it
6.1.10

Summary and key terms

  • starch test: boil, ethanol, iodine; destarch first
  • foil, variegated leaves and soda lime show the need for light, chlorophyll and CO₂
  • light, CO₂ and temperature can each limit the rate (Supp.)
Destarch
keep a plant in the dark so it uses up its starch
Variegated
a leaf with green parts (with chlorophyll) and white parts (without)
Limiting factor
the factor in shortest supply, which controls the rate (Supp.)

6.2Plant nutrition

The leaf: a factory one hand thick

A leaf is broad to catch light, thin so gases have little distance to travel, and layered so the cells packed with chloroplasts sit right under the surface.

1

Understand

Three sections

6.2.1

The layers of a leaf

TissueDescription and job
Waxy cuticlewaterproof layer on top; cuts water loss by evaporation
Upper epidermisthin, transparent cells with no chloroplasts — light passes straight through
Palisade mesophylltall cells packed with chloroplasts, near the top where light is strongest — most photosynthesis happens here
Spongy mesophyllrounded, loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases diffuse quickly
Vascular bundle (vein)xylem brings water and mineral ions; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids away
Lower epidermiscontains stomata, each between two guard cells that open and close it
6.2.2

How a leaf is adapted for photosynthesis

  • large surface area — catches more light
  • thin — short diffusion distance for carbon dioxide; light reaches all the cells
  • stomata — let CO₂ in and O₂ out; guard cells close them to save water
  • air spaces in the spongy layer — CO₂ reaches every cell
  • chloroplasts concentrated in the palisade layer, close to the light
  • veins — supply water and carry products away
6.2.3

Summary and key terms

  • cuticle → upper epidermis → palisade → spongy (air spaces) → lower epidermis with stomata
  • broad, thin, many chloroplasts, stomata, air spaces, veins
Stoma (stomata)
a pore in the leaf epidermis, opened and closed by guard cells
Guard cells
a pair of cells around a stoma that control its size
Mesophyll
the middle tissue of a leaf, where photosynthesis happens
