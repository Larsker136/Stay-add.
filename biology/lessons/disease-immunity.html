10.1Diseases & immunity

Pathogens and how they travel

Bacteria, viruses, fungi and protoctists that cause disease spread from person to person in a few predictable ways — and each way can be blocked.

1

Understand

Four sections

10.1.1

Pathogens and disease

  • a pathogen is a disease-causing organism — a bacterium, virus, fungus or protoctist
  • a transmissible disease is one in which the pathogen can be passed from one host to another
  • the pathogen may be passed by direct contact (through blood or other body fluids) or indirectly — from contaminated surfaces, food, animals or air
10.1.2

How pathogens spread

RouteExamples
Direct contact with blood and body fluidsHIV (sex, shared needles), hepatitis B
Contaminated food or watercholera, Salmonella food poisoning
Air: droplets from coughs and sneezesinfluenza, colds, tuberculosis, COVID-19
Contaminated surfacesdoor handles, towels, cutting boards
Animals (vectors)mosquitoes carry the malaria protoctist; houseflies carry bacteria onto food
10.1.3

Controlling the spread

  • clean water supply — treated with chlorine and filtered
  • hygienic food preparation — washing hands, cooking thoroughly, keeping raw and cooked food apart, refrigeration
  • good personal hygiene — washing hands, covering coughs
  • waste disposal — refuse collected and buried or burned so it does not attract flies and rats
  • sewage treatment — so human waste does not contaminate drinking water
10.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protoctists
  • spread by body fluids, food and water, air, surfaces, vectors
  • controlled by clean water, food hygiene, personal hygiene, waste disposal and sewage treatment
Pathogen
a disease-causing organism
Transmissible disease
a disease in which the pathogen can be passed from one host to another
Vector
an animal that carries a pathogen from one host to another

10.1Diseases & immunity

Three lines of defence

Skin and mucus keep most pathogens out; stomach acid kills those that are swallowed; and white blood cells destroy any that get into the blood.

1

Understand

Four sections

10.1.5

Mechanical and chemical barriers

TypeBarrierHow it helps
Mechanicalskina tough outer layer of dead cells that pathogens cannot get through; scabs seal wounds
Mechanicalhairs in the nosetrap particles and pathogens in the air breathed in
Chemicalmucussticky; traps pathogens in the airways — cilia sweep it up to be swallowed
Chemicalstomach acidhydrochloric acid kills pathogens in food and drink
10.1.6

Cells: phagocytes and lymphocytes

  • phagocytes engulf pathogens and digest them with enzymes — phagocytosis
  • lymphocytes make antibodies
10.1.7

Antibodies Supp.

  • every pathogen has antigens on its surface — molecules with a particular shape
  • an antibody is a protein with a binding site whose shape is complementary to one specific antigen
  • antibodies bind to antigens and either destroy the pathogen directly or mark it so phagocytes find and engulf it
  • each antibody fits only one antigen, so a different antibody is needed for each pathogen
10.1.8

Summary and key terms

  • skin, nose hairs, mucus and stomach acid keep pathogens out
  • phagocytes engulf; lymphocytes make antibodies
  • antibodies are specific to antigens (Supp.)
Antigen
a molecule on the surface of a pathogen that triggers an immune response (Supp.)
Antibody
a protein made by lymphocytes that binds to a specific antigen
Phagocytosis
engulfing and digesting a pathogen

10.1Diseases & immunity

Immunity: the body remembers

The second time a pathogen arrives, memory cells make antibodies so fast that you never feel ill. Vaccines give you that first meeting without the disease.

1

Understand

Five sections

10.1.9

Active immunity Supp.

Definition Active immunity is defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body.

  • gained after an infection by a pathogen or by vaccination
  • lymphocytes that recognise the antigen divide to make many copies; some make antibodies, some become memory cells
  • memory cells stay in the blood for years; on a second infection they respond faster and make more antibodies — the pathogen is destroyed before symptoms appear
  • it is long-term
10.1.10

How vaccination works

  1. a vaccine contains harmless pathogens or their antigens (dead or weakened pathogens, or parts of them)
  2. the antigens trigger an immune response: lymphocytes make antibodies
  3. memory cells are produced and give long-term immunity
  • if most people are vaccinated, the pathogen cannot spread, protecting even those who cannot be vaccinated — herd immunity; this controls the spread of disease in a population
10.1.11

Passive immunity Supp.

  • passive immunity is short-term defence by antibodies made by another individual
  • examples: antibodies crossing the placenta to a fetus, and in breast milk to a baby; antibody injections
  • no memory cells are made, so it is short-lived
  • breastfeeding matters because a baby’s own immune system is still developing
10.1.12

Cholera Supp.

  1. the cholera bacterium is spread in water contaminated with sewage
  2. in the small intestine it releases a toxin
  3. the toxin causes chloride ions to be secreted into the gut
  4. this lowers the water potential in the gut, so water moves out of the blood by osmosis
  5. the result is severe diarrhoea, dehydration and loss of salts from the blood

Treatment: oral rehydration solution (water, glucose and salts).

10.1.13

Summary and key terms

  • vaccine → antigens → antibodies + memory cells → long-term protection
  • active immunity: own antibodies, memory cells, long-term; passive: someone else’s antibodies, short-term (Supp.)
  • cholera toxin → chloride ions into gut → water follows by osmosis → diarrhoea (Supp.)
Vaccine
harmless pathogens or antigens that trigger immunity
Memory cell
a lymphocyte that remains after an infection and responds fast to the same antigen (Supp.)
Active immunity
defence by antibodies made in the body (Supp.)
Passive immunity
short-term defence by antibodies made by another individual (Supp.)
