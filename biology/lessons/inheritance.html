17.1Inheritance

Chromosomes, genes and proteins

Every cell carries the full instructions for building you, written in DNA. A gene is one instruction: the order of its bases spells out the order of amino acids in one protein.

1

Understand

Seven sections

17.1.1

Chromosomes, genes and alleles

  • a chromosome is a thread-like structure in the nucleus, made of DNA, carrying genetic information in the form of genes
  • a gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein — for example an enzyme, a hormone, or a structural protein such as keratin in hair
  • an allele is an alternative form of a gene: the gene for eye colour can come as an allele for brown eyes or an allele for blue eyes
  • chromosomes come in pairs, one from each parent, so you have two copies of most genes — two alleles, which can be the same or different

Keep the levels apart chromosome (a long DNA molecule) ⊃ gene (one section of it) ⊃ allele (one version of that gene).

17.1.2

Haploid and diploid nuclei Supp.

  • human body cells have 46 chromosomes: 23 pairs, two copies of each kind
  • a nucleus with two sets of chromosomes in pairs is diploid
  • gametes have one chromosome from each pair — 23 in humans: a haploid nucleus

diploid human cell: 2n = 46 · haploid human gamete: n = 23

Tip haploid = half. Questions may use other species — always work from the number you are given.

17.1.3

The inheritance of sex

Sex is decided by one whole pair of chromosomes, the sex chromosomes: females are XX, males are XY.

  • every egg carries an X, because the mother only has X chromosomes
  • half of the sperm carry an X and half carry a Y — so the sperm decides the sex of the child
  • each child has a 50 % chance of being a girl and a 50 % chance of being a boy

17.1.4

The genetic code Supp.

DNA is two strands twisted into a double helix, joined by pairs of bases. There are four bases, and they always pair the same way: A with T, C with G.

  • the sequence of bases in a gene decides the sequence of amino acids in the protein it codes for
  • the bases are read in groups of three; each triplet stands for one amino acid
  • a different order of amino acids folds into a different shape, and the shape decides what the protein does — the active site of an enzyme, the binding site of an antibody

Don’t mix them up DNA and RNA are chains of bases; proteins are chains of amino acids.

17.1.5

Protein synthesis Supp.

DNA stays in the nucleus — it is far too big to pass through the pores in the nuclear membrane. Proteins are made at ribosomes in the cytoplasm. A messenger carries the code between them.

  1. in the nucleus, the base sequence of a gene is copied into a molecule of messenger RNA (mRNA)
  2. the mRNA leaves the nucleus and attaches to a ribosome
  3. the ribosome reads the mRNA three bases at a time
  4. for each triplet, the matching amino acid is brought to the ribosome and joined to the growing chain, in the order the code gives
  5. the finished chain is released and folds into the protein’s shape

gene (DNA, nucleus) → mRNA → ribosome reads triplets → amino acids joined in order → protein folds

17.1.6

Which proteins are made? Supp.

  • almost every body cell contains the same genes — a full copy of the DNA
  • but each cell only expresses (switches on) the genes for the proteins it needs; most genes in any one cell are switched off
  • so a cell in the pancreas makes insulin and a muscle cell does not, even though both carry the insulin gene
  • in this way DNA controls what a cell does — through which enzymes, antibodies or receptors (for example, for neurotransmitters) it makes
17.1.7

Summary and key terms

  • chromosomes are made of DNA; a gene codes for a protein; alleles are versions of a gene
  • XX female, XY male; the sperm decides; 50 % chance of each
  • base sequence → amino acid sequence → protein shape and job; made via mRNA at ribosomes (Supp.)

17.2Inheritance

Mitosis: two identical cells

You grew from one cell into trillions, and you replace millions every second. Each time, the nucleus copies every chromosome and shares the copies out exactly — so every new cell has the same genes.

1

Understand

Four sections

17.2.1

What mitosis is, and what it is for

Mitosis is nuclear division giving rise to genetically identical cells.

RoleExample
Growtha zygote divides again and again to become an embryo, then a whole organism
Repair of damaged tissuescells at the edge of a cut divide to close the wound
Replacement of cellsskin cells, red blood cells and cells lining the gut are worn out and replaced constantly
Asexual reproductionthe offspring are made by mitosis, so they are clones (16.1)
17.2.2

Explaining mitosis Supp.

  1. before the cell divides, every chromosome is copied exactly (replicated). The two copies stay joined, so each chromosome now looks like an X
  2. the chromosomes line up across the middle of the cell
  3. fibres pull the two copies of each chromosome apart, to opposite ends of the cell
  4. a new nucleus forms at each end, and the cytoplasm divides: two cells

Because each new cell receives one copy of every chromosome, it has the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell (46 in humans; still diploid) and exactly the same genes.

exact copying → separation of the copies → two genetically identical diploid cells

17.2.3

Stem cells Supp.

Stem cells are unspecialised cells that divide by mitosis to produce daughter cells that can become specialised for specific functions.

  • the zygote divides by mitosis into a ball of identical, unspecialised cells — embryonic stem cells, which can become almost any kind of cell
  • as the embryo develops, cells specialise into muscle, nerve, blood cells and so on
  • adults keep a few stem cells in some tissues — for example in bone marrow, where they make new blood cells — but these can form fewer kinds of cell
17.2.4

Summary and key terms

  • mitosis: one division, two genetically identical diploid cells
  • used for growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction
  • chromosomes are copied exactly first, then the copies separate (Supp.)
Mitosis
nuclear division giving rise to genetically identical cells
Replication

Stem cell
an unspecialised cell that divides by mitosis to make cells that can specialise (Supp.)

17.3Inheritance

Meiosis: four different gametes

Gametes need half the usual chromosomes, so that fertilisation brings the number back to normal. Meiosis halves it — and shuffles which chromosome of each pair goes where, so the gametes differ.

1

Understand

Five sections

17.3.1

Where meiosis happens, and why

  • meiosis is the division that makes gametes
  • in humans it happens in the testes (sperm) and the ovaries (eggs); in flowering plants in the anthers (pollen) and the ovules
  • the gametes are haploid, so fertilisation restores the diploid number in the zygote
  • the gametes are genetically different from each other and from the parent cell
17.3.2

Explaining meiosis Supp.

Meiosis is a reduction division in which the chromosome number is halved from diploid to haploid, resulting in genetically different cells.

  1. every chromosome is copied, as before mitosis
  2. the chromosomes line up in their pairs, side by side, across the middle of the cell
  3. first division: the members of each pair are pulled apart into two cells. Each cell now has one chromosome from each pair — the number is halved
  4. second division: the copies of each chromosome are pulled apart, as in mitosis
  5. result: four haploid cells, which develop into gametes
17.3.3

Where the variation comes from

  • when the pairs line up, which member of each pair (the one from the mother or the one from the father) faces which side is random, and each pair lines up independently
  • with 2 pairs there are 2² = 4 kinds of gamete; with the 23 pairs in humans there are 2²³ — about 8 million
  • sections of a pair can also swap places while the pairs are lined up, which adds even more combinations
  • then fertilisation is random too: any sperm can meet any egg — so each child is a new combination

17.4Inheritance

Monohybrid inheritance: predicting offspring

With two letters per parent and a four-square grid you can predict the chance of a child having a feature — and work backwards from a family tree to find out who carries a hidden allele.

Seven sections

The language of inheritance

Phenotype

Heterozygous

Letters use a capital for the dominant allele and the same letter in lower case for the recessive one. Pick a letter whose cases look different when handwritten — A/a or T/t, not C/c or S/s.

You cannot always tell the genotype from the phenotype: a tall plant could be TT or Tt. A heterozygous individual is not pure-breeding, because it can pass on either allele.

17.4.2

Monohybrid crosses and Punnett squares

  1. choose letters for the alleles (or use the ones given)
  2. write the genotypes of both parents
  3. write the gametes each parent can make — one allele each — along the top and side
  4. fill each box with one allele from its column and one from its row
  5. write the phenotype for each box, then give the ratio or probability asked for

Probability, not a promise A 3 : 1 ratio is the chance for each offspring. In a small family the real numbers often differ — try sampling in the lab.

17.4.3

Pedigree analysis

A pedigree diagram (family tree) traces a characteristic, usually a genetic disorder, through the generations of a family.

  • squares are males, circles are females; shaded symbols are affected individuals
  • a horizontal line joining a male and a female shows they are parents; their children hang below

Working out the type of allele

  • two unaffected parents with an affected child: the allele must be recessive, and both parents must be heterozygous (carriers) — each passed on a hidden recessive allele
  • for a dominant allele, every affected child has at least one affected parent
  • an affected individual with a recessive condition must be homozygous recessive (aa)

17.4.5

Codominance: blood groups Supp.

Codominance is when both alleles in a heterozygous individual contribute to the phenotype. The ABO blood groups are the example to know.

  • the gene has three alleles: IA, IB and IO, but each person has only two
  • IA and IB are codominant; both are dominant to IO
  • IA makes antigen A on red blood cells, IB makes antigen B, IO makes neither

17.4.6

Sex linkage Supp.

A sex-linked characteristic is one where the gene is on a sex chromosome — almost always the X, because the Y is small and carries few genes.

  • write the alleles on the X: XB (normal colour vision, dominant) and Xb (red–green colour blindness, recessive); the Y carries no allele
  • a male has only one X, so a single recessive allele is enough: XbY is colour-blind. That is why such conditions are much more common in males
  • a female needs two: XbXb. A female with XBXb has normal vision but is a carrier — she can pass the allele on
  • haemophilia is inherited the same way

Summary and key terms

  • genotype → phenotype; dominant shows with one copy, recessive needs two
  • Punnett squares give ratios: Tt × Tt → 3 : 1; Tt × tt → 1 : 1
  • two unaffected parents with an affected child → recessive, both carriers
  • test crosses, codominance (ABO) and sex linkage (XbY) (Supp.)
