21.1Biotechnology

Tiny factories

Bacteria multiply fast, make complex molecules, and carry their genes on easily swapped plasmids. That makes them ideal workers for biotechnology and genetic engineering.

1

Understand

Two sections

21.1.1

Why bacteria are useful

  • they reproduce rapidly — numbers can double every 20 minutes or so
  • they can make complex molecules such as proteins and enzymes
  • Supp. there are few ethical concerns over growing and manipulating them
  • Supp. they have plasmids — small rings of DNA that are easy to take out, change and put back
  • Supp. they share the same genetic code as all organisms, so they can read and express a human gene
21.1.2

Summary and key terms

  • bacteria grow fast and make complex molecules
  • plasmids, a shared genetic code and few ethical issues make them ideal for genetic modification (Supp.)
Biotechnology
using living organisms, especially microorganisms, to make useful products
Plasmid
a small ring of DNA in a bacterium, separate from its chromosome

21.2Biotechnology

Yeast, enzymes and fermenters

Yeast makes bread rise and fuel burn; enzymes clear fruit juice and clean clothes; huge steel fermenters grow microorganisms that make antibiotics and insulin.

1

Understand

Four sections

21.2.1

Yeast: biofuels and bread

  • biofuels: yeast respires sugar (from crops such as sugar cane or maize) anaerobically to make ethanol, which can be used as fuel
  • bread: yeast in dough respires anaerobically, making carbon dioxide that forms bubbles and makes the dough rise; baking kills the yeast and evaporates the ethanol

21.3Biotechnology

Moving a gene from one species to another

Cut the human insulin gene out, paste it into a bacterial plasmid, and the bacterium makes human insulin. The same idea gives crops that resist pests or carry extra vitamins.

1

Understand

Four sections

21.3.1

What genetic modification is

Definition Genetic modification is changing the genetic material of an organism by removing, changing or inserting individual genes.

  • inserting human genes into bacteria to make human proteins, such as insulin
  • inserting genes into crop plants to give herbicide resistance, insect resistance or improved nutritional quality (e.g. golden rice with extra vitamin A)
21.3.2

Making human insulin Supp.

  1. the human insulin gene is cut out of human DNA with a restriction enzyme, leaving sticky ends (short single-stranded lengths)
  2. a bacterial plasmid is cut with the same restriction enzyme, giving matching sticky ends
  3. the gene and plasmid are joined at their sticky ends by DNA ligase — making recombinant plasmids
  4. the recombinant plasmids are inserted into bacteria
  5. bacteria that took up the plasmid are grown in a fermenter; they multiply and express the gene, making human insulin, which is extracted and purified
21.3.3

21.3.4

Summary and key terms

  • GM changes an organism’s genes by removing, changing or inserting them
  • restriction enzyme cuts; ligase joins; recombinant plasmid into bacteria; fermenter (Supp.)
  • GM crops: higher yields and nutrition, but concerns about biodiversity and cost (Supp.)
Genetic modification
changing the genetic material of an organism by removing, changing or inserting individual genes
Restriction enzyme
an enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific base sequence, leaving sticky ends (Supp.)
DNA ligase
an enzyme that joins DNA fragments (Supp.)
Recombinant plasmid
a plasmid containing an inserted gene from another organism (Supp.)
