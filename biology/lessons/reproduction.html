16.1Reproduction

Asexual reproduction: copies of one parent

A bacterium splits in two, a potato sprouts new plants, a strawberry sends out runners. No partner is needed — and every offspring carries exactly the same genes as its parent.

1

Understand

Four short sections

16.1.1

What asexual reproduction is

Asexual reproduction is a process resulting in the production of genetically identical offspring from one parent.

  • no gametes (sex cells) are made and there is no fertilisation, so genetic information from two parents is never mixed
  • the new cells are produced by mitosis (17.2), which copies every chromosome exactly
  • the offspring are therefore genetically identical to the parent and to each other: they are clones
  • clones can still look different — one grown in the shade may be smaller — but those differences come from the environment, not from their genes

one parent → mitosis → genetically identical offspring (clones)

16.1.2

Examples to recognise

Exam questions often show a diagram or a short description and ask whether it is asexual. Look for one parent and no gametes.

OrganismHow it reproducesWhat you see
BacteriaBinary fission: the cell copies its DNA and splits into twotwo identical daughter cells; in good conditions some bacteria divide every 20 minutes or so
YeastBudding: a small outgrowth forms on the parent cellthe bud grows and breaks off as a new cell
PotatoTubers: swollen underground stems full of stored starchbuds (the “eyes”) sprout into new plants the next year
Onion, daffodilBulbs: short stems wrapped in fleshy, food-storing leavesside bulbs grow and separate into new plants
StrawberryRunners: side shoots that grow along the groundsmall plantlets on the runner put down roots and become separate plants

Bulbs and tubers are food stores: the new plant lives on the stored starch until its leaves can photosynthesise.

16.1.3

Advantages and disadvantages Supp.

Whether copying is a good strategy depends on whether conditions stay the same.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
In the wildonly one parent is needed, so no time or energy is spent finding a mate · the population grows quickly and can fill a good habitat fastno variation, so a new disease, predator or change in climate affects every individual in the same way · offspring grow close to the parent and compete for light, water and nutrients
For cropsevery plant has the parent’s useful features (high yield, disease resistance) · the crop is uniform, which suits harvesting and selling · often quicker and cheaper than growing from seedthe crop cannot adapt if the climate changes · one disease or pest can destroy the whole crop · a diseased parent passes the disease on · it needs human work, such as taking cuttings

Remember the trade-off in one line: asexual reproduction is fast and reliable but gives no variation.

  • examples: binary fission in bacteria, budding in yeast, potato tubers, bulbs, strawberry runners

16.2Reproduction

Sexual reproduction: a new mix every time

Two parents each give half of their chromosomes. Which half is down to chance, so brothers and sisters are never exactly alike — and that variation is what helps a species survive change.

1

Understand

Four short sections

16.2.1

Gametes, fertilisation and the zygote

Sexual reproduction is a process involving the fusion of the nuclei of two gametes to form a zygote, and the production of offspring that are genetically different from each other.

  • a gamete is a sex cell: in animals the sperm and the egg cell (ovum); in flowering plants the nucleus inside a pollen grain and the egg cell inside an ovule
  • fertilisation is the fusion of the gamete nuclei — not just the cells meeting
  • the fertilised egg cell is the zygote; it divides by mitosis to grow into the new organism
  • each gamete carries a different selection of its parent’s chromosomes (meiosis, 17.3), so every zygote is a new combination

gamete (parent 1) + gamete (parent 2) → fertilisation → zygote → offspring, all genetically different

16.2.2

Haploid and diploid Supp.

A normal body cell has chromosomes in pairs. A gamete has only one of each pair.

  • a haploid nucleus has one set of chromosomes — half the usual number. Human gametes have 23
  • a diploid nucleus has two sets, in pairs. The human zygote has 46, or 23 pairs: half from the mother, half from the father
  • the zygote, the embryo and almost every cell of the body that grows from it stay diploid
  • halving in the gametes is what keeps the number constant: without it, the chromosome number would double every generation

haploid sperm (n = 23) + haploid egg (n = 23) → diploid zygote (2n = 46)

Watch out for other species in questions: a dog has 78 chromosomes in its body cells, so its gametes have 39. Always halve the number you are given.

16.3Reproduction

Flowers: built to move pollen

A plant cannot walk to a partner, so its male gametes travel inside pollen grains — carried by insects or by the wind. Every part of a flower is shaped by which one it relies on.

1

Understand

Eight sections

16.3.1

Insect-pollinated flowers

The flower you probably picture — big petals, a scent, nectar — is built to bring insects in. The male parts are the stamens; the female part is the carpel.

PartMale / femaleFunction
Sepal—protects the flower while it is still a bud
Petal—large and brightly coloured to attract insects
Anthermale (stamen)makes pollen grains, which contain the male gametes
Filamentmale (stamen)a stalk that holds the anther in position
Stigmafemale (carpel)sticky surface at the top of the carpel that catches pollen
Stylefemale (carpel)joins the stigma to the ovary
Ovaryfemale (carpel)contains and protects the ovules; becomes the fruit
Ovulefemale (carpel)contains the female gamete; becomes the seed

Many insect-pollinated flowers also have nectaries at the base of the petals. They make sugary nectar, a reward that keeps insects coming back.

16.3.2

Pollination

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from an anther to a stigma. A pollen grain carries the male gamete nucleus, so pollination is how the male gamete makes its journey.

  • insect pollination: an insect pushes into the flower for nectar, brushes against the anthers and picks up pollen, then rubs it onto the stigma of the next flower it visits
  • wind pollination: pollen is shaken out of hanging anthers and blown about; a tiny fraction lands on a stigma of the same species
  • pollen grains differ to match: insect-pollinated plants make larger, sticky or spiky grains that cling to insects; wind-pollinated plants make huge numbers of small, light, smooth grains, sometimes with air sacs or “wings” that catch the air

Careful pollination is not fertilisation. Pollination moves pollen; fertilisation is the fusion of nuclei, which happens later inside the ovule.

16.3.3

Wind or insect?

Every difference makes sense once you ask: does this flower need to attract an animal, or catch the air?

FeatureInsect-pollinatedWind-pollinated
Petalslarge, brightly colouredsmall and dull (green or brown), or absent
Scent and nectarpresent, to attract insectsabsent — making them would waste energy
Anthersinside the flower, on stiff filaments, so insects brush against themhang outside the flower on long, flexible filaments
Stigmainside the flower, small and stickylarge and feathery, outside the flower, to trap pollen from the air
Pollenlarger, sticky or spiky; less of itsmall, light and smooth; made in huge amounts

Grasses, wheat and maize are wind-pollinated; most garden flowers are insect-pollinated.

16.3.4

Practical: dissecting a flower

Taking a flower apart from the outside in shows how its parts are arranged — and how many of each there are.

Method

  1. look at the whole flower first and draw it, labelling what you can see
  2. with forceps, remove the sepals, then the petals, from the outside inwards; lay each kind out in a row on paper and count them
  3. remove the stamens and count them; look at an anther with a hand lens to see the pollen
  4. leave the carpel: with a scalpel, cut the ovary in half lengthways and use a hand lens to see the ovules inside
  5. stick each set of parts onto paper with clear tape and label them

Safety

Cut on a tile, away from your fingers. Some people are allergic to pollen.

Do the same with a grass flower and compare: no bright petals, dangling anthers and feathery stigmas.

16.3.5

  • self-pollination: pollen lands on a stigma of the same flower, or another flower on the same plant
  • cross-pollination: pollen lands on a stigma of a different plant of the same species

16.3.6

Fertilisation, seeds and germination

Fertilisation happens when a pollen nucleus fuses with the nucleus of the female gamete in an ovule.

  1. Supp. a pollen tube grows out of the pollen grain, down through the style to the ovary
  2. the male nucleus travels down the tube — the pollen grain itself stays on the stigma
  3. the tube enters an ovule through a small hole (the micropyle) and the nuclei fuse: fertilisation
  4. the fertilised ovule becomes a seed and the ovary becomes the fruit; petals, stamens and the style wither and fall off

Germination

Germination is the start of growth of a seed into a seedling. A seed needs three conditions:

  • water — the seed swells, its tissues soften, and enzymes become active to break down stored food
  • oxygen — for aerobic respiration, which releases the energy for growth
  • a suitable temperature — warmth speeds up the enzyme-controlled reactions (too hot denatures the enzymes; see Topic 5)

Light is not needed: the seed lives on its stored food until its first leaves can photosynthesise. Carbon dioxide is not needed either.

16.3.7

Practical: what does a seed need to germinate?

Change one condition at a time and keep everything else the same. The figure in step 2 shows the set-up.

Method

  1. put the same number of seeds (for example 10 cress seeds) on cotton wool in four tubes
  2. A: dry cotton wool, 20 °C · B: damp cotton wool, 20 °C (the control) · C: damp cotton wool, 20 °C, but the oxygen is removed from the air in the sealed tube by a chemical · D: damp cotton wool, in a fridge at about 4 °C
  3. leave for a few days, then count how many seeds have germinated in each tube

Results and conclusion

Only B germinates. A lacks water, C lacks oxygen and D is too cold: all three conditions are needed.

16.3.8

Summary and key terms

  • stamen = anther + filament (male); carpel = stigma + style + ovary with ovules (female)
  • insect- and wind-pollinated flowers differ in petals, scent, nectar, anthers, stigmas and pollen
  • pollination → fertilisation (pollen tube, Supp.) → ovule becomes seed, ovary becomes fruit
  • germination needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature

16.4Reproduction

Humans: from two gametes to a fetus

Millions of sperm set off; one reaches the egg in the oviduct. From that single cell, nine months of growth follow — fed and protected by the placenta and the fluid around it.

16.4.2

Fertilisation and the gametes

Fertilisation is the fusion of the nucleus of a sperm with the nucleus of an egg cell. It happens in the oviduct.

  • sperm swim from the vagina, through the cervix and uterus, into the oviducts; only a tiny fraction get that far
  • one sperm gets through the jelly coat and its nucleus enters the egg; the nuclei fuse to make a zygote
  • the jelly coat then changes so no other sperm can get in

16.4.3

Pregnancy: from zygote to fetus

  1. the zygote moves along the oviduct towards the uterus, dividing by mitosis into a ball of cells: the embryo
  2. a few days later the embryo sinks into the thick uterus lining: implantation. This is the start of pregnancy
  3. the main organs form in the first weeks; once they are all in place, the developing baby is called a fetus, and it spends the rest of the pregnancy growing
  4. a human pregnancy (the gestation period) lasts about 9 months
  • the fetus floats in amniotic fluid inside the amniotic sac; the fluid cushions it against bumps and knocks
  • the umbilical cord joins the fetus to the placenta, which is attached to the uterus wall
16.4.4

The placenta and umbilical cord Supp.

In the placenta the fetus’s blood flows very close to the mother’s blood — but the two never mix. Substances cross by diffusion.

DirectionSubstances
Mother → fetusoxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, water, mineral ions (and some antibodies)
Fetus → mothercarbon dioxide, urea
  • the placenta has a large surface area and a thin barrier, so diffusion is fast
  • it stops many toxins and pathogens, but not all: nicotine and other drugs, and some viruses such as rubella and HIV, can cross — one reason pregnant women are advised not to smoke
  • the umbilical cord carries the fetus’s blood to and from the placenta

Exam tip Name specific substances. “Nutrients” and “waste” earn no marks; “glucose” and “urea” do.

  • zygote → embryo → implants in the uterus lining → fetus, protected by amniotic fluid and supplied by the placenta

16.5Reproduction

Sex hormones: puberty and the monthly cycle

Hormones from the testes and ovaries change the body at puberty. In women, four hormones then run a repeating 28-day cycle: build up the uterus lining, release an egg, and keep the lining ready for a possible pregnancy.

1

Four sections

16.5.1

Puberty and secondary sexual characteristics

Primary sexual characteristics are the reproductive organs you are born with. Secondary sexual characteristics develop at puberty, caused by sex hormones.

The menstrual cycle

The cycle starts at puberty and lasts about 28 days. Day 1 is the first day of a period.

16.5.3

Hormones and the menstrual cycle Supp.

Four hormones control the cycle: two from the pituitary gland in the brain, two from the ovaries.

HormoneMade inEffect

  1. FSH from the pituitary gland makes a follicle develop in the ovary
  2. the follicle makes oestrogen, which builds up the uterus lining
  3. when oestrogen peaks, the pituitary releases a surge of LH, which causes ovulation
  4. the empty follicle becomes the corpus luteum and makes progesterone, which keeps the lining thick
  5. no pregnancy: the corpus luteum breaks down, progesterone falls, and the lining is shed — menstruation
  6. pregnancy: the corpus luteum keeps making progesterone until the placenta takes over, so the lining is kept

Graphs On a hormone graph, ovulation is where LH peaks. Progesterone is high in the second half of the cycle; its fall comes just before a period.

16.6Reproduction

Sexually transmitted infections: HIV

Some pathogens pass from person to person in body fluids during sex. HIV is the one to know: it attacks the very cells that should fight it, and over years it can lead to AIDS.

1

Understand

Three sections

16.6.1

STIs and how HIV spreads

  • STIs are caused by pathogens that pass in semen, vaginal fluids or blood during unprotected sex
  • some STIs are caused by bacteria, such as chlamydia and gonorrhoea, and can be treated with antibiotics; HIV is a virus, so antibiotics do not work against it
  • HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) can lead to AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome)
RouteHow HIV passes
Sexual contactunprotected sex with an infected person
Bloodsharing needles (for example, between drug users); transfusion of infected blood — rare where donated blood is tested
Mother to childacross the placenta, during birth, or in breast milk

HIV is not passed on by touching, hugging, sharing cups or by insect bites.

16.6.2

How HIV affects the immune system

  1. soon after infection there may be a short, mild illness like flu; then often no symptoms for years, so people may not know they are infected
  2. HIV infects a type of lymphocyte; inside, it uses the cell to make many copies of itself, and the lymphocyte is destroyed
  3. HIV keeps changing its surface proteins, which makes it hard for the immune system to recognise and destroy
  4. over the years the number of lymphocytes falls, so fewer antibodies can be made
  5. the body can no longer fight off infections that a healthy immune system would deal with easily, such as tuberculosis and pneumonia: this stage is AIDS

HIV infects lymphocytes → fewer lymphocytes → fewer antibodies → other infections take hold → AIDS

There is no cure or vaccine, but drugs can keep the amount of virus very low, so people with HIV can live long lives and are much less likely to pass it on.

  • STIs pass in body fluids during sex; HIV can also spread through blood and from mother to child
  • HIV destroys lymphocytes, so fewer antibodies are made and other infections take hold: AIDS
  • condoms, fewer partners, testing and treatment, clean needles and education control the spread
