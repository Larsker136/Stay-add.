1.1Classification

Seven things every living thing does

A crystal can grow and a car can move, but neither is alive. Living organisms do all seven of the life processes — and it is the whole set that counts.

1

Understand

Three sections

1.1.1

MRS GREN: the seven characteristics

To decide whether something is alive, check it against all seven. A mnemonic helps: MRS GREN.

LetterCharacteristicExample
MMovementa cheetah runs; a sunflower turns to follow the Sun
RRespirationevery cell releases energy from glucose
SSensitivityyou pull your hand away from a hot plate
GGrowtha seedling gets taller and heavier
RReproductionbacteria divide; humans have children
EExcretionyou breathe out carbon dioxide; kidneys remove urea
NNutritionplants photosynthesise; animals eat food
1.1.2

The definitions examiners want

Movement
an action by an organism or part of an organism causing a change of position or place
Respiration
the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism
Sensitivity
the ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment
Growth
a permanent increase in size and dry mass
Reproduction
the processes that make more of the same kind of organism
Excretion
the removal of the waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements
Nutrition
the taking in of materials for energy, growth and development

Watch the words Growth is a permanent increase in dry mass — a sponge soaking up water is not growing. Excretion removes metabolic waste; getting rid of undigested food (egestion) is not excretion.

1.1.3

Summary and key terms

  • living organisms show all seven characteristics: movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, nutrition
  • non-living things may show one or two (a fire “grows” and “uses oxygen”) but never all seven
Organism
a single living thing — one bacterium, one oak tree, one person
Metabolism
all the chemical reactions that happen in a living organism

1.2Classification

Sorting life into groups

There are millions of species. Classification puts them into groups by shared features, gives each a name every scientist understands, and lets us identify an unknown organism with a key.

1

Understand

Five sections

1.2.1

Species, and groups within groups

  • a species is a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring — a horse and a donkey can breed, but the mule is infertile, so they are different species
  • organisms are classified into groups by the features they share
  • the groups get smaller and more alike at each level: kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species

King Philip Came Over For Good Soup — kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species

1.2.2

The binomial system

Common names change from place to place, so every species has a two-part scientific name: the genus then the species.

  • humans are Homo sapiens; the lion is Panthera leo and the tiger Panthera tigris — same genus, so closely related
  • the genus starts with a capital, the species with a lower-case letter; printed in italics or underlined when handwritten

Definition The binomial system is an internationally agreed system in which the scientific name of an organism is made up of two parts, showing the genus and the species.

1.2.3

Dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key identifies an organism through a series of questions, each with two possible answers. Each answer leads to the next question or to a name.

  • use features you can see and that do not change: number of legs, wings or not, shape of leaves — not size or colour, which vary
  • when you build a key, split the group into two at each step until each organism stands alone; you need one fewer question than organisms
StepQuestionGo to
1legs present? yes → 2 · no → earthworm
2more than 8 legs? yes → centipede · no → 3
38 legs? yes → spider · no (6 legs) → 4
4wings? yes → fly · no → ant (worker)
1.2.4

Classification, evolution and DNA Supp.

  • classification tries to reflect evolutionary relationships: organisms in the same group share a common ancestor
  • older systems used only body features; today scientists also compare the base sequences in DNA and the amino acid sequences in proteins
  • the more similar the sequences, the more closely related the species, and the more recently they shared an ancestor
  • DNA evidence has moved some species — fungi, for example, turn out to be more closely related to animals than to plants
1.2.5

Summary and key terms

  • organisms are grouped by shared features; the groups nest from kingdom down to species
  • binomial name = Genus species; keys identify organisms by pairs of choices
  • DNA base sequences show how closely species are related (Supp.)
Species
a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring
Binomial system
the two-part (genus and species) scientific naming system
Dichotomous key
a key that identifies organisms by a series of two-way choices
Common ancestor
an ancestral species from which two or more later species evolved (Supp.)

1.3Classification

Five kingdoms, and a virus that fits none

All living things share some features — and differ in others. Cell walls, a nucleus, chloroplasts: a few questions place any organism in one of five kingdoms.

1

Understand

Four sections

1.3.1

What all organisms share

  • every living organism is made of cells with cytoplasm, a cell membrane, ribosomes (for making proteins) and DNA as its genetic material
  • they all use enzymes and respire to release energy
1.3.2

The five kingdoms

KingdomMain featuresExamples
Animalmulticellular; nucleus; no cell wall, no chloroplasts; feed on organic substances made by other organismsinsects, fish, humans
Plantmulticellular; nucleus; cellulose cell wall; chloroplasts — make their food by photosynthesismosses, ferns, flowering plants
Fungususually multicellular (yeast is single-celled); nucleus; cell wall not of cellulose; no chloroplasts; feed by saprotrophic nutrition — secrete enzymes onto dead matter and absorb the productsmushrooms, moulds, yeast
Protoctistmostly single-celled; nucleus; some plant-like with chloroplasts, some animal-likeAmoeba, Plasmodium, algae
Prokaryotesingle-celled; no nucleus — DNA is a loop in the cytoplasm, often with plasmids; cell wall (not cellulose); no mitochondriabacteria

Remember Only prokaryotes lack a nucleus. Fungi have cell walls but are not plants: no chloroplasts, and their walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.

1.3.3

Viruses Supp.

  • viruses are not placed in any kingdom: they are not made of cells and show none of the life processes on their own
  • a virus is a protein coat around genetic material — DNA or RNA
  • they can only reproduce inside a host cell, using the cell’s ribosomes and enzymes to make copies of themselves
  • far smaller than bacteria — they can only be seen with an electron microscope
1.3.4

Summary and key terms

  • all organisms: cells with cytoplasm, membrane, ribosomes, DNA
  • animal, plant, fungus, protoctist (eukaryotes, with a nucleus) and prokaryote (no nucleus)
  • viruses: protein coat + DNA or RNA; reproduce only in host cells (Supp.)
Prokaryote
an organism whose cell has no nucleus
Eukaryote
an organism whose cells have a nucleus
Saprotrophic
feeding by secreting enzymes onto dead organic matter and absorbing the digested products
Plasmid
a small ring of DNA found in bacteria, separate from the main loop

1.3Classification

Backbones, or a skeleton on the outside

Two big animal groups come up again and again: the vertebrates, with a backbone, and the arthropods, with jointed legs and an exoskeleton. A few features sort each one into its classes.

1

Understand

Three sections

1.3.5

The five classes of vertebrates

ClassSkinOther key features
Mammalsfur or hairexternal ears (pinnae); mammary glands make milk; most give birth to live young; whiskers
Birdsfeathersbeak; wings (two legs, two wings); lay eggs with hard shells
Reptilesdry scaleslay eggs with rubbery, leathery shells on land
Amphibiansmoist skin, no scaleslay eggs in water; larvae (tadpoles) live in water with gills, adults on land with lungs

Skin first The quickest sort is by body covering: fur → mammal, feathers → bird, dry scales → reptile, moist skin → amphibian, wet scales with fins → fish.

Summary and key terms

  • vertebrates: mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish — sorted by skin and reproduction
  • arthropods: insects (6 legs), arachnids (8), crustaceans (10+), myriapods (many)

Spores, seeds and leaf veins

Ferns spread by spores; flowering plants make seeds inside flowers. Among the flowering plants, the leaf veins and the number of petals split them into two groups.
