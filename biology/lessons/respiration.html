12.1Respiration

Respiration releases energy

Respiration is not breathing. It is a set of chemical reactions in every living cell that releases the energy stored in glucose — energy for moving, growing, and keeping warm.

1

Understand

Four sections

12.1.1

What respiration is

Definition Respiration is the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism.

  • it happens in every living cell, all the time
  • reactions are controlled by enzymes
  • breathing (ventilation) only moves air in and out; respiration is what happens inside cells
12.1.2

What the energy is used for

  • muscle contraction
  • protein synthesis — joining amino acids
  • cell division and growth
  • active transport
  • the passage of nerve impulses
  • maintaining a constant body temperature in mammals and birds
12.1.3

Temperature and the rate of respiration

  • respiration is controlled by enzymes, so temperature affects its rate
  • germinating seeds or yeast can be used to show this: measure oxygen uptake with a respirometer, or CO₂ release
  • the rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, then falls as enzymes denature

Respirometer

Germinating seeds in a sealed tube with soda lime (which absorbs CO₂). As the seeds take in oxygen, the gas volume falls and a coloured liquid moves along a capillary tube towards the seeds. A control tube has glass beads (or boiled seeds) instead.

12.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • respiration releases energy from nutrients in every cell
  • energy for muscles, protein synthesis, division, growth, active transport, nerve impulses, warmth
Respiration
the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism
Respirometer
apparatus that measures the rate of respiration, usually as oxygen uptake

12.2Respiration

Aerobic: glucose plus oxygen

With oxygen, glucose is broken down completely to carbon dioxide and water in the mitochondria — releasing a lot of energy.

1

Understand

Three sections

12.2.1

Aerobic respiration

Definition Aerobic respiration is the chemical reactions in cells that use oxygen to break down nutrient molecules to release energy.

glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O (Supp.)

It is the reverse of the photosynthesis equation — but it releases energy instead of storing it.

12.2.2

Where it happens

  • most of aerobic respiration happens in the mitochondria
  • very active cells — muscle cells, sperm, cells doing active transport — have many mitochondria
  • oxygen comes from the lungs via red blood cells; glucose from digestion via plasma
  • CO₂ diffuses out of the cell into the blood and is breathed out
12.2.3

Summary and key terms

  • glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water; C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O (Supp.)
  • happens in mitochondria; releases much energy
Aerobic respiration
respiration that uses oxygen
Mitochondrion
the organelle where aerobic respiration releases energy

12.3Respiration

Anaerobic: when oxygen runs out

Without oxygen, cells can still release a little energy from glucose. Muscles make lactic acid; yeast makes alcohol and carbon dioxide — the basis of bread and brewing.

1

Understand

Four sections

12.3.1

Anaerobic respiration

Definition Anaerobic respiration is the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules to release energy without using oxygen.

in muscles: glucose → lactic acid

in yeast: glucose → alcohol (ethanol) + carbon dioxide

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ (Supp.)

  • yeast’s CO₂ makes bread rise; its ethanol is used in brewing and biofuel production
12.3.2

Compared with aerobic respiration

AerobicAnaerobic
Oxygenneedednot needed
Productscarbon dioxide + waterlactic acid (muscles), or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast)
Energy released per glucosemuch moremuch less — glucose is only partly broken down
12.3.3

Lactic acid and oxygen debt Supp.

  • during vigorous exercise, muscles cannot get oxygen fast enough, so they also respire anaerobically, and lactic acid builds up in muscles and blood
  • this causes fatigue and cramp
  • afterwards there is an oxygen debt: extra oxygen is needed to break down the lactic acid
  • the lactic acid is carried in the blood to the liver, where it is respired aerobically (converted back to glucose)
  • so after exercise you keep breathing fast and deep, and the heart rate stays high, to supply this oxygen and carry lactic acid away
12.3.4

Summary and key terms

  • muscles: glucose → lactic acid; yeast: glucose → ethanol + CO₂
  • anaerobic releases much less energy per glucose
  • oxygen debt: oxygen needed to break down lactic acid in the liver (Supp.)
Anaerobic respiration
respiration without oxygen
Lactic acid
the product of anaerobic respiration in muscle
Oxygen debt
the extra oxygen needed after exercise to remove lactic acid (Supp.)
