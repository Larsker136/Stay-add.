2.1Organisation

Inside a cell

Every organism is built from cells. Animal and plant cells share a basic kit — nucleus, cytoplasm, membrane — and plants add three extras. Bacteria do the same jobs with a much simpler design.

PartFunction

2.1.3

Bacterial cells

Bacteria are prokaryotes: much smaller than animal or plant cells, with no nucleus.

  • a cell wall (not made of cellulose), a cell membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes
  • DNA as one circular chromosome loose in the cytoplasm, and often small rings called plasmids
  • no mitochondria and no chloroplasts; some have a flagellum for movement
2.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • both animal and plant cells: membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, ribosomes, mitochondria
  • plant cells add a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts and a large permanent vacuole
  • bacteria: no nucleus; circular DNA and plasmids
Partially permeable
lets some substances through but not others
Cell sap
the solution of sugars and salts in a plant cell’s vacuole
Plasmid
a small ring of DNA in a bacterium

2.1Organisation

Each cell shaped for its job

In a multicellular organism, cells specialise. Their shapes match their functions — and similar cells group into tissues, tissues into organs, organs into systems.

2.1.6

Cells → tissues → organs → organ systems

Cell
the basic unit of life — a muscle cell
Tissue
a group of cells with similar structures, working together to perform a shared function — muscle tissue
Organ
a structure made of a group of tissues, working together to perform specific functions — the stomach, a leaf
Organ system
a group of organs with related functions, working together to perform body functions — the digestive system
Organism
the whole living thing, made of several organ systems

Plants too Xylem and phloem are tissues; a leaf, stem or root is an organ.

2.1.7

Summary and key terms

  • specialised cells have structures that suit their function
  • cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism
Specialised cell
a cell with features adapted to one particular function
Differentiation
the process by which a cell becomes specialised

2.2Organisation

How big is it really?

Cells are too small to see, so we draw and photograph them magnified. One formula links the image, the real thing and the magnification — as long as the units match.

1

Understand

Four sections

2.2.1

The magnification formula

magnification = image size ÷ actual size

  • rearrange as needed: image size = actual size × magnification; actual size = image size ÷ magnification
  • a magnification has no units — write it as ×400
  • image size and actual size must be in the same units before you divide
2.2.2

Millimetres and micrometres Supp.

1 mm = 1000 µm · mm → µm: × 1000 · µm → mm: ÷ 1000

  • cells are usually measured in micrometres (µm): a typical animal cell is 10–30 µm across
  • images on paper are measured with a ruler in millimetres — so convert one of them
2.2.3

Worked examples

  1. A drawing of a cell is 60 mm wide. The cell is really 0.03 mm wide. Magnification = 60 ÷ 0.03 = ×2000.
  2. A photo at ×400 shows a cell 12 mm long. Actual size = 12 ÷ 400 = 0.03 mm = 30 µm.
  3. A chloroplast is 5 µm long. Drawn at ×5000, image = 5 × 5000 = 25 000 µm = 25 mm.

Check Does the answer make sense? A cell measured in metres, or a magnification of ×0.02, means a unit went wrong.

2.2.4

Summary and key terms

  • magnification = image size ÷ actual size; no units
  • convert to the same units first; 1 mm = 1000 µm
Magnification
how many times bigger the image is than the real object
Micrometre (µm)
one thousandth of a millimetre
