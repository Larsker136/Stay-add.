14.1Coordination

Nerves: fast electrical messages

Receptors detect a change, neurones carry impulses to the brain or spinal cord, and effectors respond — in a reflex, before you have even thought about it.

1

Understand

Five sections

14.1.1

The nervous system

  • the central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord
  • the peripheral nervous system (PNS) is the nerves outside it
  • it coordinates and regulates body functions; messages travel as electrical impulses along neurones
  • receptor → sensory neurone → CNS → motor neurone → effector (a muscle or gland)
14.1.2

Three types of neurone

NeuroneCarries impulsesShape
Sensoryfrom receptors to the CNSlong dendron; cell body part-way along
Relaywithin the CNS, from sensory to motor neuronesshort, many branches
Motorfrom the CNS to effectorscell body at one end; long axon
14.1.3

Reflex arcs

  • a reflex action is a means of automatically and rapidly integrating and coordinating stimuli with the responses of effectors
  • it is fast, automatic (no conscious thought) and protects the body — pulling away from heat, blinking, the pupil reflex

stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (spinal cord) → motor neurone → effector → response

14.1.4

Synapses Supp.

  1. an impulse arrives at the end of the first neurone
  2. vesicles release a neurotransmitter into the narrow gap — the synaptic cleft
  3. the neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap
  4. it binds to receptor proteins on the next neurone’s membrane, starting a new impulse
  • synapses make sure impulses travel in one direction only: only the first neurone releases transmitter, only the second has receptors
  • many drugs (e.g. heroin) act on synapses by copying or blocking neurotransmitters
14.1.5

Summary and key terms

  • CNS = brain + spinal cord; impulses are electrical
  • sensory → relay → motor neurones in a reflex arc
  • neurotransmitter diffuses across a synapse; one-way (Supp.)
Stimulus
a change in the environment that can be detected
Receptor
a cell or organ that detects a stimulus
Effector
a muscle or gland that responds
Synapse
a junction between two neurones (Supp.)

14.2Coordination

The eye: a camera that adjusts itself

The cornea and lens focus light onto the retina; the iris controls how much gets in. Both adjust automatically for bright or dim light and for near or far objects.

Rods and cones Supp.

  • rods are very sensitive to light — they work in dim light but give only black-and-white vision; found all over the retina except the fovea
  • cones give colour vision; three types sensitive to red, green and blue light; need brighter light; packed into the fovea

14.3Coordination

Hormones: slower messages in the blood

Endocrine glands release hormones straight into the blood. They reach every organ but only affect target organs — more slowly than nerves, but for longer.

1

Understand

Four sections

14.3.1

Endocrine glands and their hormones

Definition A hormone is a chemical substance, produced by a gland and carried by the blood, which alters the activity of one or more specific target organs.

GlandHormoneEffect
Adrenal glandsadrenalineprepares the body for “fight or flight”

14.3.2

Adrenaline

  • released in situations of fear, stress or excitement
  • increases breathing rate and heart rate; widens the pupils
  • Supp. raises blood glucose concentration (liver breaks down glycogen) — more glucose and oxygen reach muscles for respiration, ready for action

  • adrenaline: faster heart and breathing, wider pupils, more blood glucose (Supp.)
  • nerves: fast, short, precise; hormones: slow, long, widespread

14.4Coordination

Homeostasis: keeping things steady

Your body holds its temperature near 37 °C and its blood glucose within narrow limits. A change is detected, a response pushes it back — negative feedback.

1

Understand

Five sections

14.4.1

Homeostasis and negative feedback

Definition Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment.

  • body temperature, blood glucose, water and pH are kept within narrow limits so cells and enzymes work properly
  • Supp. negative feedback: when a factor moves away from its set point, the change is detected and a response brings it back
14.4.2

Controlling blood glucose

  • the pancreas monitors blood glucose concentration
  • too high (after a meal): the pancreas releases insulin; the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen and store it — blood glucose falls
  • Supp. too low (exercise, fasting): the pancreas releases glucagon; the liver converts glycogen back to glucose — blood glucose rises
14.4.3

Type 1 diabetes Supp.

  • the pancreas cannot make enough insulin (the cells that make it have been destroyed)
  • symptoms: high blood glucose, glucose in the urine, thirst, tiredness, weight loss
  • treatment: insulin injections, monitoring blood glucose, and a controlled diet with regular meals and exercise
14.4.4

Controlling body temperature

  • the skin contains hairs, sweat glands, receptors, blood vessels and fatty tissue (which insulates)
  • Supp. the hypothalamus in the brain detects blood temperature and coordinates the responses
Too hotToo cold
sweating — evaporation of sweat removes heatno sweating
vasodilation Supp.: arterioles near the skin widen, more blood flows near the surface and loses heat by radiationvasoconstriction Supp.: arterioles narrow, less blood near the surface, less heat lost
hairs lie flathairs stand up, trapping a layer of insulating air
—shivering: muscles contract rapidly; respiration releases heat
14.4.5

Summary and key terms

  • homeostasis keeps the internal environment constant; negative feedback (Supp.)
  • insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it (Supp.)
  • hot: sweat, vasodilation; cold: shiver, vasoconstriction, hairs up
Homeostasis
the maintenance of a constant internal environment
Insulin
a hormone from the pancreas that lowers blood glucose
Glucagon
a hormone from the pancreas that raises blood glucose (Supp.)
Vasodilation
widening of arterioles near the skin surface (Supp.)

14.5Coordination

Plants grow towards what they need

Shoots bend towards light; roots grow down with gravity. A hormone called auxin makes cells on one side grow longer than the other.

1

Understand

Three sections

14.5.1

Gravitropism and phototropism

Gravitropism
a response in which parts of a plant grow towards or away from gravity
Phototropism
a response in which parts of a plant grow towards or away from the direction of light
  • shoots: positive phototropism (towards light — for photosynthesis) and negative gravitropism (upwards)
  • roots: positive gravitropism (downwards — for water, minerals and anchorage)
  • investigate with seedlings in a box lit from one side, or on a clinostat that slowly rotates to cancel out gravity
14.5.2

How auxin works Supp.

  1. auxin is a plant hormone made in the shoot tip
  2. it diffuses down through the plant
  3. in a shoot lit from one side, auxin collects on the shaded side
  4. auxin stimulates cell elongation, so cells on the shaded side grow longer
  5. the shoot bends towards the light

Synthetic auxins are used as weedkillers: they make broad-leaved weeds grow too fast and die, but do not affect narrow-leaved crops like wheat.

14.5.3

Summary and key terms

  • shoots grow towards light and against gravity; roots grow with gravity
  • auxin collects on the shaded side and makes those cells elongate (Supp.)
Auxin
a plant hormone made in shoot tips that stimulates cell elongation (Supp.)
Clinostat
a slowly turning disc that removes the effect of one-sided gravity or light
