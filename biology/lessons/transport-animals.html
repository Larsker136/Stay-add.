9.1Transport in animals

One loop or two?

The circulatory system is a heart, tubes and valves that keep blood flowing one way. A fish’s blood passes through the heart once per circuit; ours passes through twice.

1

Understand

Four sections

9.1.1

The circulatory system

A system of blood vessels with a pump (the heart) and valves to make sure blood flows in one direction. It carries oxygen, glucose, CO₂, urea, hormones and heat around the body.

9.1.2

Single circulation in fish

  • heart → gills → rest of body → heart
  • blood passes through the heart once for each complete circuit
  • after the gills the blood is at low pressure, so it flows slowly to the body
9.1.3

Double circulation in mammals

  • blood passes through the heart twice per circuit
  • pulmonary circulation: right side of the heart → lungs → left side (picks up oxygen)
  • systemic circulation: left side → body → right side (delivers oxygen)

Advantages Supp. Blood returns to the heart after the lungs and is pumped again at high pressure, so it reaches the body tissues quickly — delivering oxygen and glucose fast enough for a high metabolic rate. Lower pressure to the lungs avoids damaging the delicate capillaries there.

9.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • fish: single circulation; mammals: double circulation
  • double circulation keeps pressure high for the body (Supp.)
Single circulation
blood passes through the heart once per circuit
Double circulation
blood passes through the heart twice per circuit — to the lungs, then to the body

9.2Transport in animals

The heart: two pumps side by side

Four chambers, four valves, and a muscular wall that never rests. The left ventricle is thickest — it has to push blood round the whole body.

1

Understand

Five sections

9.2.1

Structure of the heart

PartRole
Right atriumreceives deoxygenated blood from the body through the vena cava
Right ventriclepumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs through the pulmonary artery
Left atriumreceives oxygenated blood from the lungs through the pulmonary vein
Left ventriclepumps oxygenated blood to the body through the aorta; has the thickest muscle wall
Septumwall between the two sides — stops oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mixing
Atrioventricular valvesbetween atria and ventricles (tricuspid on the right, bicuspid on the left); stop backflow into the atria
Semilunar valvesat the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery; stop backflow into the ventricles
Coronary arteriessupply the heart muscle itself with oxygen and glucose

Thickness Supp. Ventricles have thicker walls than atria (they pump further). The left ventricle is thicker than the right: it pumps at higher pressure all round the body, while the right only pumps to the nearby lungs.

9.2.2

How the heart pumps Supp.

  1. blood flows into both atria from the veins
  2. the atria contract, pushing blood through the open atrioventricular valves into the ventricles
  3. the ventricles contract: the atrioventricular valves snap shut (preventing backflow), and blood is forced out through the semilunar valves into the arteries
  4. the ventricles relax; the semilunar valves close so blood cannot flow back in

The closing valves make the “lub-dub” sound of the heartbeat.

9.2.3

Heart rate and exercise

  • heart activity can be monitored by an ECG, by taking the pulse (at the wrist or neck), or by listening to the valves with a stethoscope
  • during exercise the heart rate increases
  • Supp. muscles respire faster and need more oxygen and glucose, and make more CO₂; a faster heart rate delivers more blood to the muscles and removes CO₂ faster
  • after exercise the rate gradually returns to normal; fitter people recover faster
9.2.4

Coronary heart disease

  • fatty deposits build up in the coronary arteries, narrowing them; a blood clot may block them completely
  • heart muscle beyond the blockage gets too little oxygen and glucose, cannot respire, and dies — a heart attack
  • risk factors: diet high in saturated fat, lack of exercise, smoking, stress, being overweight, genetic predisposition, age and being male
  • Supp. prevention: balanced diet, regular exercise, not smoking; treatment: aspirin to reduce clotting, stents to hold arteries open, angioplasty (a balloon widens the artery), bypass surgery using a vessel from elsewhere
9.2.5

Summary and key terms

  • right side: deoxygenated blood to lungs; left side: oxygenated blood to body
  • valves stop backflow; septum stops mixing; left ventricle thickest
  • exercise raises heart rate; CHD blocks coronary arteries

9.3Transport in animals

Arteries, veins and the capillaries between

Arteries take high-pressure blood away from the heart, veins bring low-pressure blood back, and capillaries — one cell thick — are where exchange with the tissues happens.

  • Supp. arteries: elastic tissue stretches and recoils with each heartbeat, smoothing the flow and withstanding high pressure
  • Supp. veins: wide lumen offers less resistance to slow, low-pressure blood; valves stop backflow; nearby muscles squeeze veins to push blood back
  • Supp. capillaries: thin, leaky walls give a short diffusion distance; huge networks give a large surface area for exchange; tissue fluid forms here
  • Supp. shunt vessels can carry blood directly from an arteriole to a venule, bypassing a capillary bed — for example, near the skin when the body needs to keep heat in

9.4Transport in animals

Blood: a liquid tissue

More than half of blood is a straw-coloured liquid, plasma. Suspended in it are red cells that carry oxygen, white cells that fight disease, and platelets that seal wounds.

9.4.3

Clotting Supp.

  • a clot prevents blood loss and stops pathogens entering through a wound
  • at a wound, platelets trigger a series of reactions that convert the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin
  • fibrin forms a mesh of threads that traps red blood cells — the clot, which dries into a scab

wound → platelets → fibrinogen (soluble) → fibrin (insoluble mesh) → clot

  • fibrinogen → fibrin mesh traps cells (Supp.)
