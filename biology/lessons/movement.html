3.1Movement in & out

Diffusion: spreading out

Molecules never stop moving. Where they are crowded, more of them happen to wander away than wander in — so they spread out, with no energy needed from the cell.

1

Understand

Four sections

3.1.1

What diffusion is

Definition Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, as a result of their random movement.

  • the energy comes from the kinetic energy of the particles’ own random movement — the cell spends none (it is passive)
  • “net” matters: particles move both ways, but more move from high to low
  • it continues until the concentrations are equal; then movement carries on but there is no net change
3.1.2

Diffusion in living things

  • substances move into and out of cells by diffusion through the cell membrane
  • gas exchange: oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the blood; carbon dioxide diffuses out
  • leaves: carbon dioxide diffuses in through the stomata; oxygen and water vapour diffuse out
  • small intestine: some digested food molecules diffuse into the blood in the villi
  • placenta: oxygen and glucose diffuse to the fetus; urea and carbon dioxide diffuse back
3.1.3

What speeds diffusion up Supp.

Concentration gradientsteeper → fastera bigger difference means more particles moving one way than the other

Practical Agar cubes containing an indicator, placed in acid: the colour change spreads inward. Smaller cubes (larger surface area to volume ratio) change completely faster.

3.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • diffusion: net movement from high to low concentration, by random movement; no energy from the cell
  • faster with a steeper gradient, higher temperature, larger surface area and shorter distance
Concentration gradient
the difference in concentration between two regions
Passive process
one that needs no energy from respiration
Surface area : volume ratio
surface area divided by volume; it falls as an object gets bigger

3.2Movement in & out

Osmosis: water follows the solutes

A membrane that lets water through but not sugar decides which way water moves. Plant cells firm up or go floppy; animal cells swell and burst, or shrivel.

1

Understand

Six sections

3.2.1

What osmosis is

  • water moves into and out of cells by osmosis through the partially permeable cell membrane
  • water molecules are small enough to pass through; larger solute molecules (such as sugar) are not
  • water moves from a dilute solution (lots of water) to a concentrated solution (less water)
3.2.2

Osmosis and water potential Supp.

Definition Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane.

  • water potential is how freely water molecules can move; pure water has the highest
  • adding solute lowers the water potential, because water molecules cluster around the solute
3.2.3

Plant cells: turgid, flaccid, plasmolysed

Placed inWater movesResult
Pure water / dilute solutioninto the cellvacuole swells and pushes against the wall: turgid. The strong cell wall stops it bursting
Solution of equal concentrationno net movementno change
Concentrated solutionout of the cellvacuole shrinks; cell goes flaccid; if more water is lost the membrane and cytoplasm pull away from the wall: plasmolysis
  • turgor pressure — the push of the cell contents on the wall — keeps cells rigid and supports non-woody plants; when cells lose it, the plant wilts Supp.
3.2.4

Animal cells

  • no cell wall, so in a dilute solution water enters until the cell bursts (red blood cells: haemolysis)
  • in a concentrated solution water leaves and the cell shrinks and becomes crenated
  • this is why body fluids — blood plasma, tissue fluid — must be kept at the right concentration
3.2.5

Practicals

Potato cylinders in sugar solutions

  1. cut equal potato cylinders; blot and record the mass of each
  2. place each in a different sucrose concentration (0 to 1.0 mol/dm³) for 30 minutes
  3. blot and reweigh; calculate percentage change in mass = (change ÷ starting mass) × 100
  4. plot % change against concentration; where the line crosses zero, the solution matches the cells

Visking tubing filled with sugar solution and placed in water swells and becomes firm — a model of a turgid cell.

3.2.6

Summary and key terms

  • osmosis is a special case of diffusion — water only, through a partially permeable membrane
  • plant cells: turgid in water, plasmolysed in concentrated solution; animal cells burst or shrink
Partially permeable membrane
a membrane that lets small molecules such as water through but not larger ones
Turgid
swollen and firm, as water has entered the vacuole
Flaccid
soft, because the cell has lost water
Plasmolysis
the cell membrane and cytoplasm pulling away from the cell wall after water loss

3.3Movement in & out

Active transport: uphill, with energy

Sometimes a cell needs more of a substance than there is outside. Carrier proteins in the membrane pump it in against the gradient, paid for with energy from respiration.

1

Understand

Four sections

3.3.1

What active transport is

Definition Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against a concentration gradient, using energy released during respiration.

  • root hair cells take in mineral ions such as nitrate from the soil, where they are more dilute than inside the cell
  • the small intestine absorbs glucose and amino acids into the villi even when their concentration in the gut is low
  • cells that do a lot of active transport have many mitochondria
3.3.2

Carrier proteins Supp.

  1. a particle binds to a specific carrier protein in the membrane
  2. energy from respiration changes the carrier’s shape
  3. the particle is moved to the other side and released; the carrier returns to its original shape

Anything that stops respiration — no oxygen, a poison such as cyanide, low temperature — stops active transport.

  • active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration
