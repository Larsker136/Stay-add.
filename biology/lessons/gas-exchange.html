11.1Gas exchange

Alveoli: where air meets blood

Air travels down a branching tree of tubes to 300 million tiny air sacs. Their walls are one cell thick and wrapped in capillaries — the perfect surface for diffusion.

The surface is also moist, so gases dissolve before they diffuse.

11.1.2

The breathing system

nose/mouth → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli

  • the trachea is held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage so it does not collapse
  • it divides into two bronchi, one to each lung, which branch into narrower bronchioles
  • bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli
  • the lungs sit in the thorax, protected by the ribs; the intercostal muscles lie between the ribs and the diaphragm lies beneath
  • the larynx (voice box) sits at the top of the trachea
11.1.3

Keeping the airways clean Supp.

  • goblet cells in the lining of the trachea and bronchi make mucus, which traps dust and pathogens
  • ciliated cells beat their cilia to sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed
  • this protects the gas exchange surface from infection and damage
11.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • large area, thin, good blood supply, ventilated — and moist
  • trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli; cartilage keeps the trachea open
  • mucus traps, cilia sweep (Supp.)

11.1Gas exchange

Breathing: change the volume, move the air

The lungs have no muscles of their own. The diaphragm and the muscles between the ribs make the chest bigger or smaller — and air follows the pressure difference.

1

Understand

Three sections

11.1.5

Breathing in (inhalation) Supp.

  1. the external intercostal muscles contract and the internal ones relax: the ribs move up and out
  2. the diaphragm contracts and flattens
  3. the volume of the thorax increases, so the pressure inside decreases below atmospheric pressure
  4. air is drawn into the lungs
11.1.6

Breathing out (exhalation) Supp.

  1. the external intercostal muscles relax; the ribs move down and in (in forced breathing out, the internal intercostals contract to pull them down)
  2. the diaphragm relaxes and domes upward
  3. the volume of the thorax decreases, so the pressure increases above atmospheric
  4. air is pushed out of the lungs

Antagonistic pair The internal and external intercostal muscles work against each other: when one contracts, the other relaxes.

11.1.7

Summary and key terms

  • in: external intercostals contract, ribs up and out, diaphragm flattens, volume up, pressure down
  • out: muscles relax, ribs down and in, diaphragm domes, volume down, pressure up
Diaphragm
a sheet of muscle below the lungs; flattens when it contracts
Intercostal muscles
muscles between the ribs that raise and lower the ribcage
Ventilation
moving air into and out of the lungs

11.1Gas exchange

What goes in, what comes out

Breathed-out air has less oxygen, a hundred times more carbon dioxide, and more water vapour. During exercise you breathe faster and deeper to keep up.

11.1.9

Testing for carbon dioxide

  • limewater turns from clear to milky (cloudy) with carbon dioxide
  • breathe in through one tube and out through another: the limewater the expired air bubbles through goes cloudy much faster
11.1.10

Exercise and breathing

  • during exercise, the rate and depth of breathing increase
  • measure with a spirometer, or by counting breaths per minute before, during and after exercise
  • Supp. muscles respire faster, making more carbon dioxide; the brain detects the rise in CO₂ concentration in the blood and sends impulses that make breathing faster and deeper
  • Supp. this removes CO₂ faster and supplies more oxygen; after exercise breathing stays fast for a while to repay the oxygen debt (see Respiration)
11.1.11

Summary and key terms

  • expired air: less O₂, more CO₂, more water vapour, warmer
  • limewater turns milky with CO₂
  • exercise → faster, deeper breathing; triggered by rising CO₂ (Supp.)
Limewater
