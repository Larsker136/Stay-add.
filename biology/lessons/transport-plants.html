8.1Transport in plants

Two pipelines: xylem up, phloem both ways

Water and mineral ions climb from the roots in xylem; sugars and amino acids travel in phloem to wherever they are needed. The two run side by side in vascular bundles.

8.1.2

How xylem is adapted Supp.

  • made of long, dead cells joined end to end with the end walls gone — one continuous hollow tube with no cytoplasm in the way
  • walls thickened with lignin, which makes them strong and waterproof — they support the plant and do not collapse under tension
  • narrow tubes help water move up as a continuous column
8.1.3

Where xylem and phloem are

OrganArrangement
Rootin the centre: xylem as a star (or X), phloem between its arms — resists pulling as the plant sways
Stemvascular bundles in a ring near the outside: phloem to the outside, xylem to the inside — resists bending
Leafin the veins (midrib): xylem on the upper side, phloem below
8.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • xylem: water and minerals upwards, support; phloem: sucrose and amino acids, both ways
  • root: centre; stem: ring of bundles; leaf: veins, xylem above phloem
Vascular bundle
a strand of xylem and phloem together
Lignin
a strong, waterproof substance in xylem walls (Supp.)

8.2Transport in plants

From soil to xylem

Water enters root hair cells by osmosis, crosses the root cortex cell by cell, and reaches the xylem in the centre — then rises to the leaves.

1

Understand

Four sections

8.2.1

Root hair cells

  • root hair cells grow a long, thin extension into the soil
  • this gives a large surface area for absorbing water (by osmosis) and mineral ions (by active transport)
  • they have no chloroplasts — they are underground
8.2.2

The pathway of water

soil → root hair cell → root cortex cells → xylem → stem → leaf mesophyll cells

  • soil water is dilute, so it has a higher water potential than the cell sap: water enters the root hair by osmosis
  • it then moves from cell to cell across the cortex towards the xylem, down a water potential gradient
  • in the leaf, water leaves the xylem and enters the mesophyll cells
8.2.3

Showing the path with a dye

Practical

  1. stand a leafy celery stalk (or a white flower) in water with a coloured dye such as eosin
  2. after a few hours, cut a thin cross-section of the stem
  3. only the xylem is stained — showing that it carries the water; dye also reaches the veins of the leaves
8.2.4

Summary and key terms

  • root hairs: large surface area; water in by osmosis, ions by active transport
  • root hair → cortex → xylem → leaf mesophyll
Root hair cell
an epidermis cell of the root with a long extension that absorbs water and ions
Cortex
the layers of cells between the root epidermis and the xylem

8.3Transport in plants

Transpiration: the pull from the top

Water evaporates from leaf cells and escapes through the stomata. That loss pulls a continuous column of water up the xylem — and it speeds up when it is warm, windy and dry.

1

Understand

Five sections

8.3.1

What transpiration is

Definition Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves.

  1. water evaporates from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces
  2. the water vapour then diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata

The large surface area of the mesophyll cells and their wet surfaces make evaporation fast. Transpiration also cools the leaf and brings mineral ions up from the roots.

8.3.2

How water rises in the xylem Supp.

  • water lost from the leaf is replaced from the xylem, creating a pull (tension) — called the transpiration pull
  • water molecules are attracted to each other (cohesion), so the whole column of water is pulled up the xylem, from the roots
  • this is the transpiration stream: soil → root → xylem → leaf → air
8.3.3

Factors affecting the rate

FactorEffect on rateWhy Supp.
Temperature ↑fasterwater molecules have more kinetic energy, so evaporate and diffuse faster
Humidity ↑slowerless water vapour gradient between the air spaces and the air outside
Wind speed ↑fastermoving air carries water vapour away from the leaf, keeping the gradient steep
Light intensity ↑fasterstomata open wider to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis — more water vapour escapes
8.3.4

Measuring transpiration, and wilting

  • a potometer measures water uptake by a leafy shoot: as the shoot takes up water, an air bubble moves along a capillary tube; distance per minute ≈ rate of transpiration
  • cut the shoot under water and seal the joints so no air enters
  • change one factor — a fan for wind, a lamp, a plastic bag for humidity — and keep the rest the same
  • wilting Supp.: if water is lost faster than it is taken up, cells lose turgor and the leaves and stem droop; stomata close to reduce loss
8.3.5

Summary and key terms

  • water evaporates from mesophyll cells, diffuses out through stomata
  • faster when hot, windy, dry, bright; transpiration pull + cohesion lift water (Supp.)
Transpiration
the loss of water vapour from leaves
Potometer
apparatus that measures the rate of water uptake by a shoot
Cohesion
the attraction between water molecules (Supp.)
Wilting
drooping of a plant as its cells lose turgor (Supp.)

8.4Transport in plants

Translocation: from source to sink

Sugar made in the leaves travels in the phloem to roots, fruits and growing tips. Which way it flows depends on where it is made and where it is used — and that changes with the seasons.

1

Understand

Three sections

8.4.1

Sources and sinks Supp.

Definition Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids in phloem from sources to sinks.

  • a source is a part of the plant that releases sucrose or amino acids — usually leaves that are photosynthesising
  • a sink is a part that uses or stores them — roots, growing shoots, flowers, fruits, seeds, storage organs
  • sinks use sucrose for respiration or store it as starch
8.4.2

Why the same organ can be both Supp.

  • a potato tuber is a sink in summer, storing starch made from sucrose sent from the leaves
  • in spring the tuber becomes a source: starch is broken down and sucrose is sent up to grow new shoots before they have leaves
  • so phloem can carry substances up or down, unlike xylem
8.4.3

Summary and key terms

  • phloem carries sucrose and amino acids from source to sink, in both directions (Supp.)
  • some organs switch between source and sink with the seasons (Supp.)
Translocation
movement of sucrose and amino acids in phloem from sources to sinks (Supp.)
Source
part of a plant that releases sucrose or amino acids (Supp.)
Sink
part of a plant that uses or stores sucrose or amino acids (Supp.)
