19.1Organisms & environment

Energy flows; it does not cycle

Sunlight enters a food chain through plants and passes from organism to organism — with most lost as heat at every step. It never comes back.

1

Understand

Two sections

19.1.1

The Sun drives every food chain

  • the principal source of energy for biological systems is the Sun
  • plants (producers) transfer light energy into chemical energy in organic molecules by photosynthesis
  • energy then passes along the chain as animals eat plants or other animals
  • at each step, energy is lost to the environment, mainly as heat from respiration
  • so energy flows through ecosystems — it is not recycled, unlike carbon and nitrogen
19.1.2

Summary and key terms

  • Sun → producers (chemical energy) → consumers → lost as heat
  • energy flows; nutrients cycle
Producer
an organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually by photosynthesis
Consumer
an organism that gets its energy by feeding on other organisms

19.2Organisms & environment

Who eats whom

A food chain shows one path of energy; a food web shows all the tangled paths. Pyramids show numbers, biomass or energy at each level — and why chains are short.

1

Understand

Five sections

19.2.1

Food chains and food webs

  • a food chain shows the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer: grass → rabbit → fox
  • arrows point in the direction energy flows — from the organism eaten to the eater
  • a food web is a network of interconnected food chains
  • decomposers (bacteria and fungi) feed on dead organisms and waste, releasing nutrients back to the soil
  • a change in one population affects others: remove the foxes and rabbits increase, then grass decreases
  • humans affect food webs by overharvesting (e.g. overfishing) and by introducing foreign species
19.2.2

Trophic levels

Trophic levelFeedingExample
1 · Producermakes its own food by photosynthesisgrass
2 · Primary consumerherbivore — eats producersrabbit
3 · Secondary consumercarnivore — eats primary consumersfox
4 · Tertiary consumereats secondary consumerseagle

An organism can feed at more than one trophic level in a web — an omnivore is both a primary and a secondary consumer.

19.2.3

Pyramids of numbers, biomass and energy

  • a pyramid of numbers shows how many organisms are at each level — it can be inverted (one oak tree feeds thousands of caterpillars)
  • a pyramid of biomass shows the total dry mass at each level — almost always a true pyramid
  • Supp. a pyramid of energy shows the energy at each level — always a true pyramid, because energy is lost at every transfer
19.2.4

Efficiency of energy transfer Supp.

  • only about 10 % of the energy at one level is passed to the next
  • the rest is lost: heat from respiration, uneaten parts (roots, bones), undigested waste (faeces), excretion
  • so food chains rarely have more than four or five levels — there is too little energy left
  • it is more efficient for humans to eat crops than livestock fed on crops: fewer levels, less energy lost — more people can be fed from the same land

efficiency = (energy at higher level ÷ energy at lower level) × 100

19.2.5

Summary and key terms

  • producer → primary → secondary → tertiary consumer; webs link chains
  • pyramids of numbers, biomass, energy (Supp.)
  • ~10 % passed on; chains short; eating plants is more efficient (Supp.)
Food chain
a diagram showing the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer
Food web
a network of interconnected food chains
Trophic level
the position of an organism in a food chain or web
Decomposer
an organism that gets energy from dead or waste organic material
Herbivore / carnivore
an animal that gets energy by eating plants / other animals

19.3Organisms & environment

Carbon and nitrogen go round

The atoms in your body have been in plants, animals, rocks and air before. Photosynthesis, feeding, respiration, decomposition and bacteria keep them cycling.

1

Understand

Three sections

19.3.1

The carbon cycle

ProcessEffect on CO₂ in the air
Photosynthesisremoves CO₂ — carbon becomes organic compounds in plants
Feedingpasses carbon compounds along food chains
Respiration (plants, animals, decomposers)returns CO₂
Decompositiondecomposers respire, releasing CO₂ from dead organisms and waste
Fossilisationdead organisms that do not decay can become coal, oil and gas
Combustion of fossil fuels and woodreturns CO₂

Supp. Burning fossil fuels and deforestation (fewer trees to absorb CO₂, and the wood is often burnt) increase CO₂ in the atmosphere — contributing to climate change.

19.3.2

The nitrogen cycle Supp.

  • plants and animals need nitrogen to make proteins (and DNA); most organisms cannot use nitrogen gas
  • nitrogen fixation: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (in soil, and in root nodules of legumes such as peas and clover) turn N₂ into compounds plants can use; lightning also fixes nitrogen
  • plants absorb nitrate ions and make amino acids and proteins; animals get proteins by feeding
  • decomposition: decomposers break down proteins in dead organisms and urea in waste into ammonium ions
  • nitrification: nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions to nitrite then nitrate ions
  • denitrification: denitrifying bacteria (in waterlogged soil, without oxygen) turn nitrate back into nitrogen gas
19.3.3

Summary and key terms

  • carbon: photosynthesis removes CO₂; respiration, decomposition and combustion return it
  • nitrogen: fixation, absorption, feeding, decomposition, nitrification, denitrification (Supp.)
Decomposition
the breakdown of dead organisms and waste by decomposers
Nitrogen fixation
conversion of nitrogen gas into nitrogen compounds (Supp.)
Nitrification
conversion of ammonium ions to nitrate ions by bacteria (Supp.)
Denitrification
conversion of nitrate ions to nitrogen gas by bacteria (Supp.)

19.4Organisms & environment

Populations grow, then level off

A few yeast cells in fresh sugar solution multiply slowly, then explosively — until food runs short and waste builds up. The S-shaped curve shows every stage.

1

Understand

Three sections

19.4.1

Populations and what limits them

Population
a group of organisms of one species, living in the same area at the same time
Community
all the populations of different species in an ecosystem (Supp.)
Ecosystem
a unit containing the community of organisms and their environment, interacting together (Supp.)

Population size is affected by food supply, predation and disease (and by competition, space and waste).

19.4.2

The sigmoid growth curve Supp.

PhaseWhat is happening
Lagfew individuals; they are adjusting and maturing — slow growth
Exponential (log)plenty of food and space; birth rate far above death rate — numbers double rapidly
Stationaryfood runs short, waste builds up, disease spreads; birth rate = death rate
Deathresources exhausted or toxic waste too high; death rate > birth rate

The human population has grown exponentially in recent centuries, thanks to better food production, medicine and sanitation.

19.4.3

Summary and key terms

  • population size depends on food, predation and disease
  • lag → exponential → stationary → death (Supp.)
Population
organisms of one species in the same area at the same time
Sigmoid curve
the S-shaped growth curve of a population (Supp.)
