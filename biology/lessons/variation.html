18.1Variation & selection

No two the same

Individuals of one species differ. Some differences fall into clear groups; others range smoothly from one extreme to the other. Genes, the environment, and mutation all play a part.

18.1.2

Genetic and environmental causes

  • genetic variation comes from the alleles inherited — and from meiosis, random fertilisation and mutation
  • environmental variation comes from conditions: diet, sunlight, exercise, accidents — a plant in shade grows taller and paler
  • environmental changes are not passed on to offspring
18.1.3

Mutation

  • a mutation is a genetic change — the only source of new alleles
  • Supp. a gene mutation is a random change in the base sequence of DNA
  • mutations happen naturally; the rate is increased by ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, UV) and some chemicals (e.g. in tobacco smoke)
  • most mutations are neutral or harmful; a few are beneficial
  • Supp. example: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a mutation in the haemoglobin gene; carriers (heterozygotes) are more resistant to malaria, so the allele is common where malaria is common
18.1.4

Summary and key terms

  • continuous: a range, genes + environment; discontinuous: groups, genes only
  • mutation creates new alleles; radiation and chemicals increase the rate

18.2Variation & selection

Built for where they live

A cactus in the desert and a water lily in a pond both photosynthesise — but one is built to save every drop of water and the other to float in it.

1

Understand

Four sections

18.2.1

Adaptive features

Definition An adaptive feature is an inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment.

Supp. More precisely: the inherited functional features of an organism that increase its fitness — the probability of that organism surviving and reproducing in the environment in which it is found.

18.3Variation & selection

Selection: who gets to breed

In nature, the individuals best suited to their environment survive and pass on their alleles. Humans do the same deliberately when they breed crops and animals.

1

Understand

Four sections

18.3.1

Natural selection

  1. there is variation within a population — partly genetic
  2. organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support
  3. so there is competition for resources — food, mates, space — and a struggle for survival
  4. individuals with features better adapted to the environment are more likely to survive
  5. they reproduce and pass on their alleles to the next generation
18.3.2

Evolution and adaptation Supp.

  • adaptation is the process, resulting from natural selection, by which populations become more suited to their environment over many generations
  • over many generations the frequency of the advantageous allele rises in the population — evolution
  • example: antibiotic resistance in bacteria (see Drugs) — the resistant bacteria survive and reproduce
  • example: the peppered moth — in sooty industrial areas, dark moths were less visible to birds on blackened trees, so they survived and became more common
18.3.3

Selective breeding (artificial selection)

  1. humans choose individuals with a desired feature
  2. they breed them together
  3. from the offspring, they again choose those showing the feature most strongly
  4. this is repeated over many generations

Examples: cows that give more milk, disease-resistant wheat, high-yield crops, dog breeds.

18.3.4

Summary and key terms

  • variation → overproduction → competition → survival of the better adapted → alleles passed on
  • over generations populations become adapted (Supp.)
  • selective breeding: humans choose parents with desired features, repeatedly
